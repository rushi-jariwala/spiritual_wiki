I was wearing the sweater out of social vanity, not warmth. At one of Gurudev's biannual offsite camps, almost everyone around me had bundled up in cardigans and woolens against the winter chill. I hadn't felt cold that morning — but I did not want to appear as though I was showing off by going without. So I wore a sweater.

*"He made me chatter and shiver with chills down my spine because I wore a sweater upon his arrival."*

By turning my pretence into reality, he demonstrated something that no lecture could have conveyed: surrender to the mahaguru includes the sacrifice of false ego, social graces, and make-believe. The sweater was not the problem. The reason for wearing it was. *(→ [[stories/mungaoli-sweater-incident|The Mungaoli Sweater]])*

Before that camp was over, he said something I have spent decades unwrapping: *Grihasth mein bhi vairagya hai.* There is renunciation within the householder life.

**What Vairagya Actually Asks**

That shiver, and what it delivered in three seconds, points to what vairagya actually demands. The word is usually translated as detachment, and the translation holds — but it reaches further than most of us assume. The first layer asks for detachment from objects, pleasures, and relationships. We understand this, even when we struggle with it. The second layer is where the practice becomes truly demanding: it asks for detachment from one's own perceived identity. From the 'I' that claims ownership of what it feels, does, and is.

> "If you aspire to higher levels of consciousness, then you need to detach not only from family, friends, and material objects but also from your own perceived identity."
> — *Aatma Sutra*, Ch.IX, p.101

These two layers are not separate steps — they inform each other. You cannot let go of external bonds deeply enough until you have begun to question who is holding on. And the moment you question the holder, the grip on what is held loosens on its own.

**The Seesaw**

There is a simple image that clarifies this. Imagine a seesaw. Joy sits on one end; sorrow on the other. Every time we attach to a pleasure — a relationship, an outcome, a version of ourselves we prefer — we load one end. The structure does the rest: the opposite end rises. Pursue joy, and you build an equal future capacity for sorrow. The seesaw swings because we set it in motion.

Vairagya does not ask you to leave the seesaw — it asks you to find the fulcrum. At the centre there is no joy, but there is also no sadness. This stillness is what the tradition calls bliss: not happiness that depends on circumstances, but an equilibrium that does not move with them. Attachment sets the seesaw swinging; detachment returns it to rest.

**He Belonged to Everyone**

To understand what this looks like when it is actually lived, I recall Gurudev. All of his followers believed he was closest to them — I included. We felt he cared for each of us far more than our parents ever had. He spent more time with us than with his own children. And yet, much later, when I came to understand him better, I realised: he belonged to everyone, and yet to no one. He was ready to relieve our pain, but not feel it.

His own words on this were precise:

> *"Main sab ka hoon aur sab mere hain.*
> *Lekin main kisi ka nahi hu aur mera koi nahi."*
> *(I belong to all, and all belong to me.*
> *Yet I belong to no one, and no one belongs to me.)*
> — Gurudev, *The Guru of Gurus*, p.108

Because he was not possessed by any particular person, he could be fully present to everyone. He role-played affection to perfection, making each of us feel uniquely loved — while the truth was that he loved everyone equally, but without an iota of emotion. What belongs to no one can be given to all. Love without emotional ownership did not diminish — it became inexhaustible, because he was not drawing from a personal reserve.

**The Googly in Gurgaon**

Understanding vairagya as a principle is one thing; living it within a household is another. For years, I came to Gurgaon every Bada Guruvar for seva at the sthan. The sessions stretched late into the night. My wife, reasonably, felt excluded. She made her complaint known — not to me, but to Gurudev.

His response came not as a reprimand but as a structure. For three years from that day, every time I came to Gurgaon on a Thursday, there was no sthan seva for me. Instead, I took my wife and children for dosas. *"With one googly, he had me for a golden duck."* *(→ [[stories/hingori-wife-dosa-correction|Hingori's Wife — Three Years of Dosas]])*

The teaching was not abstract. Vairagya within the householder life does not mean withdrawing from domestic responsibility in favour of spiritual settings that feel more elevated. The grihasth has no monastery to escape to — the household is the practice ground. Renunciation, here, means serving what is in front of you without the ego claiming the outcome of either choice.

If you must choose between the sthan and the dosa outing, know this: sometimes the dosa is the holier offering.

Know why you are wearing the sweater.
