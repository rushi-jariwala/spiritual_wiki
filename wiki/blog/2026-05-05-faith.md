There is a story I return to whenever I doubt the wisdom of acting without certainty. Gurudev wanted to purchase a 250 square yard plot adjacent to the sthan — a modest extension that would allow many more people to be served. He sent one disciple after another to negotiate the deal. Each returned without closing it. When my turn came, I walked into the owner's house knowing only one thing: I was not leaving without a signed commitment.

What happened next was entirely predictable in hindsight. The moment the owner sensed my urgency, he raised the price. I did what no negotiator would recommend: I pulled out a bundle of notes, placed them on the table, and asked if they were willing to sell. They named a figure I had no authority to offer. I shook hands on the deal anyway and returned to the sthan expecting a reprimand.

Instead, Gurudev was amused. *"I did not expect you to succeed,"* he said, *"but I am glad you did."* He explained, almost as an aside, that the other disciples had failed because they negotiated for the lowest price — missing the larger point entirely. The test was not about the money. It was about whether I would go off the beaten track in service of something that mattered.

**When Faith Binds the Guru**

I understood later that Gurudev had not been assessing my negotiation skill. He had been assessing something far harder to manufacture: the willingness to act from a trust that goes deeper than the instructions. *Guru Sutra* puts it plainly — *"Unshakeable faith in your guru steers him to an obligatory stance, binding him to your evolution. Therein lies a winning formula."* This is perhaps the most counterintuitive teaching in the tradition: that faith is not something you feel passively but something that creates an obligation in the other direction. When the disciple's faith is genuine, the guru is bound to it. He cannot look away.

Gurudev used to say that a doctor can prescribe the medication, but until the patient has faith in the doctor, the medicines will not be as effective. I saw this principle at work in the story of Dr. Shankarnarayan, a colleague at IARI who became one of Gurudev's earliest disciples. His young daughter Vaishali suffered from persistent fever and intermittent fits. When Shankarnarayan mentioned her health to Gurudev, Gurudev smiled and said nothing. Over months, Shankarnarayan's conviction grew until it crossed the line from hope into certainty. One morning, he threw away Vaishali's medicines entirely — confident she no longer needed them. She recovered completely. It was Shankarnarayan who first addressed Gurudev as *"Guruji"* — the acknowledgement, from a man trained in science, that the faith had worked where the prescriptions could not.

**Five Wasted Years**

I have no excuse for my own delay. I first met Gurudev in 1977. He blessed me and said, *"You will get a lot more than you can imagine."* I heard the words without receiving them. My arthritis improved, I was given practices to follow every Thursday, and I quietly set them aside. A year later, the arthritis returned with such ferocity that I was resorting to acupuncture twice daily.

It was only in 1982 that I truly entered — five years after that first blessing. When I finally sat before him, I had tears streaming down my face. He handed me his handkerchief and said, *"It's natural when you see your mother after a long time."* That single sentence did more for me than five years of deliberation could have. In one line, he had made himself into a mother, made my tears ordinary, and made clear that the faith I had finally arrived at was something he had been holding in reserve for me all along.

The math, Gurudev later explained from his favourite charpoy at the farm, was quite straightforward. I had wasted five years of spiritual development because my intellect had blocked the conscious expression of a faith my spirit had already formed on that very first visit. The spirit recognises what the mind takes years to admit.

**The Test That Cannot Be Prepared For**

Understanding that faith is tested is one thing; being tested is another. Gurudev tested every disciple, on various counts, sometimes by misguiding them deliberately — specifically to ascertain whether the faith was genuine or merely conditional. Conditional faith is not really faith at all. It is a transaction with favourable terms attached.

I recall the time he called me into his room and asked if I would accompany him on a trip to Europe and the United States. The conversation lasted forty-five minutes. He asked the same question in multiple ways and I kept saying I would do whatever he wished, without once saying yes. When the discussion grew inconclusive, he appeared in my dream and asked again. I heard myself reply that my destiny would not allow it. He snubbed me and said, *"If not you, let it be someone else."* I missed the trip, and understood the lesson only afterwards: *never say no to your guru*. He knows things about you that you have not yet discovered about yourself. The disciple who hedges is not being cautious — he is choosing the bank over the bridge.

**Crossing Over**

The Hebrews described faith as *"the substance of things hoped for, the evidence of things not seen."* It is a precise formulation. Faith is not the belief that everything will resolve in the way you prefer. It is the willingness to cross a gap that reason cannot measure or verify in advance.

*Guru Sutra* gives it its simplest shape:

> Exit the land of delusion
> where falsehood lives like a king.
> Let the bridge of faith
> take you places where
> your caged soul can take wing.

*(→ [[stories/guru-sutra-ch8-poem|Exit the Land of Delusion — Poem]])*

For me, faith worked like a wonder drug. I had no religious background, no knowledge of the names of Indian gods and goddesses, no conceptual framework for anything I was witnessing. A guru-bhai told me simply to believe Gurudev blindly. I did. Slowly, knowledge I did not know I possessed began to surface. Concepts I had never consciously studied arrived on their own, as if the channel had been waiting to be opened. Believing the unbelievable became easy — and then necessary.

This is the paradox that runs through all of it: you cannot engineer faith through effort alone, but without it, none of the effort connects. The bridge does not eliminate the river between where you stand and where you are going. It simply makes the crossing possible. You can stand on the bank and analyse the engineering for a lifetime. Or you can walk across.

Give the faith a chance to work.
