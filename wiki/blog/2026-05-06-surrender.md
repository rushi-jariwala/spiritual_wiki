My wallet disappeared in Gurgaon and turned up, several days later, in the pocket of my blazer at home in Mumbai.

That line still makes me smile. I had been at the sthan in Gurgaon when Gurudev suggested my wife and I fly with him to Hyderabad for a wedding. All I had was six thousand rupees in cash — plastic money was uncommon then. I sent him a message requesting we go by train instead, since the airfare was more than I could manage. I received no reply all day. The next morning, my wallet was missing. On approaching Gurudev to apologise, I saw a mischievous look in his eyes. He told me to always do as asked, and not to analyse his instructions. A few days later, I found the six thousand rupees intact in my blazer, back in Mumbai. *"Lost in Gurgaon and found in Mumbai!"* *(→ [[stories/hingori-wallet-lost-found|Lost in Gurgaon and Found in Mumbai]])*

**What Surrender Actually Means**

The word "surrender" is poorly served by its ordinary usage. When we hear it, we think of defeat — flags lowered, something irrevocably given up. A siddh guru equips his disciple to think differently. Surrender in the guru-disciple relationship is not capitulation. It is flexibility: the willingness to re-examine one's attitude, philosophy, and lifestyle, and to allow change where change is warranted. There is much to eradicate and undo before anything new can be written in. Surrender is not the end of something. It is the clearing of the page. *(→ [[concepts/surrender]])*

**The Economy of Compliance**

To understand this more clearly, consider what Gurudev asked of me in those early years. He insisted I travel from Mumbai to Gurgaon every month for bada guruvar — and not by train, but only by air. I was in a financial crunch at the time. I resisted inwardly. And then, following what I had slowly come to understand — that the guru is always right — I did as I was told. What I did not know was that Gurudev had already calculated his own arithmetic: the twenty-two hours saved by flying could be invested in seva at the sthan. The instruction was never about money; it was about time. In return, he gifted me some of the divine symbols he possessed. *An earning for eternity.* I felt foolish about my tight-fisted attitude. *(→ [[stories/hingori-dhann-airfare|Hingori's Airfare — Surrender of Dhann]])*

The surrender of the body — tann — was no less instructive. When I grew disenchanted with my family business and felt certain it was not my calling, Gurudev's instruction was quietly devastating in its simplicity: go to work, he said, even if only to read a novel in office. I objected. He repeated it. I complied. Within days I was working fourteen hours a day. The business benefited; my resentment dissolved. I had walked into his trap willingly — and found it was not a trap at all. *(→ [[stories/hingori-family-business-seva|Hingori's Family Business — Surrender of Tann]])*

**The Soul, Not the Hands**

If the surrender of wealth humbled my arithmetic, the deepest form of surrender humbled everything else.

For months during my formative years, I would travel from Mumbai to Gurgaon every bada guruvar, pay for an air ticket I could ill afford, and report for the seva shift assigned to me on the duty chart — only to find that someone else had already volunteered to cover my slot. Every time. Reluctantly I would let them. I would return to Mumbai having done nothing visible at all.

A few months later, Gurudev called me to his room and asked how I liked doing the seva. I shared my frustration. He listened in amusement. Then he said what I have never encountered in any scripture: *"Son, all I want you to do is be around, so I may use you for doing what I am doing, when I need to."* It took time to understand what he meant. When treating patients, he would meditate on us and channel our energies through his healing work. What he needed was not our hands but our spirits — available, relaxed, and relatively empty of thought. The physical task was incidental. The real seva was the availability of the soul. *(→ [[stories/hingori-seva-spirit-availability|Seva Slots Taken — Gurudev's Teaching on Spiritual Availability]])*

William Booth, founder of the Salvation Army, understood this from a very different tradition: *"The greatness of a man's power is the measure of his surrender."*

**The Blank Pages**

Understanding the depth of surrender is one thing; the surrender of the mind — of mann — is quite another. The mind resists precisely because it trusts its own logic. Our dissents and objections, at best, are baby talk against the guru's knowledge of our past, present, and future lives. Gurudev spent years working on mine — a long season of no permitted laughter, no reaction to good news, no emotional peaks or valleys, equanimity enforced at every turn. He was using his eraser. And after the eraser has done its work, there is something that needs to be rewritten on the blank pages. What Gurudev wrote there, I am still learning to read.

When the page was sufficiently clear, he placed his hand on my head and told me not to be dominated by anyone again — not by any person, not by any spiritual power. I could be respectful; I could show humility. But never subservience. *(→ [[stories/hingori-chira-self-esteem|Hingori's Chira and Self-Esteem]])* Surrender had not diminished me. It had restored me. This is the paradox the word conceals: the ego made permeable to the guru's guidance ultimately produces a disciple who stands more upright, not less.

*"Surrender to a guru is not a loss but a gain,"* the *Guru Sutra* puts it, *"a gain of self-discipline, self-counselling and an understanding of reality in the true sense."*

The wallet turns up. The earning for eternity accumulates. And eventually, you stop needing to understand the instruction before you follow it.
