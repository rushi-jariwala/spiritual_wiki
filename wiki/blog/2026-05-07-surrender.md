Every bada guruvar, I would fly from Mumbai to Gurgaon — air ticket bought at personal cost, name on the duty roster, ready to serve. And every time I arrived, the disciple already on duty would request me to let him continue through my shift as well. How was I to refuse? So I would relent, travel back, and swallow the frustration alone on the return flight.

A few months of this, and Gurudev called me to his room. He asked how I liked my seva. I told him the truth — my slot was always taken, I never actually did the seva I had been assigned. He listened with what I can only describe as amusement. Then he said: *"Son, all I want you to do is be around, so I may use you for doing what I am doing, when I need to."*

*(→ [[stories/hingori-seva-spirit-availability|Seva Slots Taken — Gurudev's Teaching on Spiritual Availability]])*

I did not fully understand it then. I know now that when Gurudev healed patients, he would meditate on us — his disciples — and channel our energies to help others. The physical duty was never the point. What he needed was our spirits: available, empty of thought, present. The body could stand in any room. The spirit was the offering.

This is what surrender in the guru-disciple tradition actually means — and it is nothing like what the word suggests.

**Not Defeat, But Flexibility**

In ordinary language, surrender implies giving up — acknowledging defeat, waving a white flag. A siddh guru equips his disciple to think differently. Surrender here means flexibility to re-examine one's attitudes, philosophy, and lifestyle. It begins with unlearning, because there is, as *Guru Sutra* puts it, "much to eradicate, undo and cleanse, before rewriting and redoing." The framework has three dimensions — tann (the body), dhann (wealth), and mann (the mind) — and each involves a different kind of release, and each returns something the releasing hand could not have imagined holding.

**The Trap**

The release of tann is where most disciples begin, and where I was most comprehensively outwitted. When I grew disenchanted with my family business, Gurudev heard my mother's complaint and gave his verdict: attend work every day, even if all I did there was read a novel. I objected. He reiterated. *Since I had surrendered myself to him and chosen never to disobey him, I did exactly as asked.* And — as I later understood — that was the trap.

Within a few days, I was so involved in what I was doing that I worked fourteen hours a day. The business benefited. What the mind had resisted was precisely what the spirit needed.

*(→ [[stories/hingori-family-business-seva|Hingori's Family Business — Surrender of Tann]])*

Lal Saheb, a disciple in Canada, cooked prasad at the sthan and helped heal visitors for more than two decades. Ladies who cooked, cleaned, and swept at the sthan for decades. Followers who visited hospitals at odd hours. *"Surrender to the guru and his mission made a saint out of him."* The results are visible in those who persist — not through dramatic events, but through the faithful repetition of ordinary acts willingly offered.

**The Earning for Eternity**

Surrender of the body is one thing. Surrender of wealth is where the mind introduces its loudest objections, because money is visible and greed is easily defended by reason. In my early years, Gurudev insisted I travel by air from Mumbai to Gurgaon each month for bada guruvar — not by the cheaper overnight train. I was in a financial crunch; my logic was solid; his instruction did not move.

I complied. He gifted me some of the divine symbols he possessed. *"The value of that is immeasurable. An earning for eternity."* I felt foolish about my earlier resistance. *Guru Sutra* states the principle plainly: money spent on oneself is a debt, while that spent in service is seva. The return on surrendered dhann is calculated in a currency the accounting mind cannot read.

*(→ [[stories/hingori-dhann-airfare|Hingori's Airfare — Surrender of Dhann]])*

**The Eraser and the Switch**

The longest surrender is that of the mind — and Gurudev began it with demolition. In my first four years as a potential disciple, I lost complete faith in my own style of thinking. He had begun to use his eraser, and the process of unlearning had started. For four years: no laughter, no joy, no reaction to good news of any kind. It was, as I once described it, almost like deadening emotions to attain boredom — and yet that boredom was a place of extraordinary equanimity. *"He had led me to a place of non-duality, or at least in that direction."* Passion turned to dispassion. Attachments thinned. All I had ever learnt until then seemed, suddenly, irrelevant.

*(→ [[stories/hingori-mind-training-non-duality|Four Years of Destroying the Mind]])*

Then, one day, Gurudev called me to his room, placed his hand on my head, and told me never to bow before any man or spiritual power again — not even the deities. I could be respectful, he said. I could show humility. But never subservience.

This is the paradox at the heart of surrender: the ego is made permeable so that something larger can move through — and what emerges is not a weakened self, but a more upright one. William Booth wrote, *"The greatness of a man's power is the measure of his surrender."* He was not speaking of defeat. He was speaking of the only reliable method of becoming, finally, more yourself.

Surrender is not what you lose. It is the most precise kind of gain.
