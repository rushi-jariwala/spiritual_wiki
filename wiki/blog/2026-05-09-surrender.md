The day Gurudev asked me to go back to work, I was not pleased. I had grown deeply disenchanted with my family business — convinced it was not my calling, and that everyone around me simply could not see what was obvious. My wife worried about the future. My mother, bless her, took it upon herself to visit Gurudev and lodge a formal complaint. After her visit, he instructed me to attend work for a few hours a day. I voiced my objection. He reiterated, patiently, that I should go to office even if it meant reading a novel there. Since I had chosen never to disobey him, I did exactly as asked. And, as I would later understand, *that was the trap.*

Within a few days, I was working fourteen hours a day.

*(→ [[stories/hingori-family-business-seva|Hingori's Family Business — Surrender of Tann]])*

Surrender — *sharanagati* — is among the most misunderstood words in the spiritual vocabulary. In common usage it implies defeat: the raised white flag, the admission of helplessness. A siddh guru asks you to think of it differently. What surrender actually requires is flexibility — the willingness to re-examine your attitudes, your assumptions, your philosophy of living, and to allow change where you had decided none was needed. *Guru Sutra* puts it precisely: "There is much to eradicate, undo, and cleanse, before rewriting and redoing." The business story I just told is a story of tann — the body's surrender. But tann is only the beginning.

**The Three Hands of Letting Go**

The tradition describes surrender as three-fold: of tann (body), dhann (wealth), and mann (the mind). Most of us experience them in that rough sequence, because tann is the easiest to offer and mann is by far the hardest. The body can be brought to the sthan for seva; the mind clings with a ferocity that makes the body's resistance seem almost trivial.

My own surrender of dhann was tested in the most unglamorous way. Gurudev had taken to suggesting I fly from Mumbai to Gurgaon every month for bada guruvar — by air, not train. The difference in cost, to someone in financial difficulty at the time, was not small. I followed the instruction, grumbling inwardly, unable to understand the logic. What arrived on the other side was not a lecture on the virtues of obedience. He gifted me some of the divine symbols he possessed.

*An earning for eternity. I felt foolish about my tight-fisted attitude.*

*(→ [[stories/hingori-dhann-airfare|Hingori's Airfare — Surrender of Dhann]])*

The wallet episode that followed was instructive in a different way. Gurudev suggested my wife and I join him on a flight to Hyderabad for a wedding. All I had was INR 6,000 in cash, and I sent him a message asking to travel by train instead. I received no reply. The next morning, my wallet was missing. On approaching him to apologise, I saw a mischievous look in his eyes. He told me I should always do as asked, and not analyse his instructions. A few days later, the INR 6,000 turned up in the pocket of my blazer, at home, in Mumbai.

*Lost in Gurgaon and found in Mumbai!*

*(→ [[stories/hingori-wallet-lost-found|Lost in Gurgaon and Found in Mumbai — The Wallet]])*

There is a logic operating in both these episodes that the ordinary mind cannot parse. Gurudev was not asking for financial sacrifice as an end in itself — he was calibrating my place in his inner circle, saving my time, and testing whether my declared faith had any real weight behind it. Kabir understood this distinction about the true master:

> *It is for the disciple to offer himself / Body and soul as a sacrifice at the Guru's feet; / But the true Master will not touch / A single penny that is offered to him.*
> — Kabir, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.9, pp.83–84

The return on surrender is always disproportionate. But it arrives only after you stop calculating.

**The Eraser**

To offer the mind — the third dimension of surrender — requires something far more radical than flying to Gurgaon once a month. The guru's first act, when a disciple genuinely hands over the mind, is not instruction but erasure. He needs to clear the pages before he can write anything true on them. All that you have accumulated over decades — opinions, habits of thought, philosophical positions, the entire architecture of your certainties — must be dismantled before a single real sentence can take root. I walked around confused for four years. I lost all faith in my own thinking. Everything I had ever learnt, until then, seemed irrelevant.

This was not madness. This was the deliberate process of lifting the veil of maya, of being inducted into non-duality. For those four years, Gurudev's teaching was simple and merciless: if you desire to conquer sorrow, you must first conquer happiness. No laughter, no joy, no reaction to good news of any kind. A boredom that, paradoxically, brought equanimity. My passion turned to dispassion, my likes and dislikes both flattened, and the engine of emotional volatility began, very slowly, to idle.

> *"There are things that your mind will never be able to understand or analyse. Always remember to do whatever you are asked without questioning it."*
> — Gurudev, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.9, p.88

**The Paradox**

Here is what no one tells you about surrender: done properly, it does not produce a diminished person. At the end of those four years, when the process was complete, Gurudev called me to his room, placed his hand on my head, and told me never to be dominated by anyone again. Not to bow before any man or spiritual power. Respectful, yes. Humble, yes. But never subservient.

I walked back into the room where a rough devotee had been in the habit of ordering me about. Something had shifted in my body language — he noticed it before I had even spoken. What had been a pattern of meekness became, in one afternoon, a posture of quiet firmness that has not left me since. *A new switch turned 'on' and my lack of confidence and meekness was turned 'off'.* Surrender had not erased me; it had cleared away everything that was not me.

*(→ [[stories/hingori-chira-self-esteem|Mohan Singh Chira — Gurudev Restores Hingori's Self-Esteem]])*

William Booth once wrote: *"The greatness of a man's power is the measure of his surrender."* When I first encountered this line, I did not fully understand it. I understand it now. The guru is not a crutch you lean on to avoid falling — he is a launch pad that, used correctly, delivers you to a flight you could not have imagined on your own. The disciple who asks the guru which television set to buy has confused one for the other.

Let him steer the wheel. The road he can see from where he stands is longer and clearer than anything your own eyes can reach.
