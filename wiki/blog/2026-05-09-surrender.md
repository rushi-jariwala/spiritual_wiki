The day Gurudev asked me to go back to work, I was not pleased. I had grown deeply disenchanted with my family business — convinced it was not my calling, and that everyone around me simply could not see what was obvious. My wife worried about the future. My mother, bless her, took it upon herself to visit Gurudev and lodge a formal complaint. After her visit, he instructed me to attend work for a few hours a day. Even if it meant reading a novel in office. Since I had chosen never to disobey him, I did exactly as asked.

Within a few days, I was working fourteen hours a day.

*(→ [[stories/hingori-family-business-seva|Hingori's Family Business — Surrender of Tann]])*

Surrender — *sharanagati* — is among the most misunderstood words in the spiritual vocabulary. In common usage it implies defeat: the raised white flag, the admission of helplessness. A siddh guru asks you to think of it differently. What surrender actually requires is flexibility — the willingness to re-examine your attitudes, your assumptions, your philosophy of living, and to allow change where you had decided none was needed. *Guru Sutra* puts it precisely: "There is much to eradicate, undo, and cleanse, before rewriting and redoing." The tradition describes this surrender as three-fold: of tann (body), dhann (wealth), and mann (mind and spirit). Most of us encounter them in that rough sequence, because tann is the most obvious to offer and mann is by far the hardest to release.

**The Surrender of Wealth**

My own surrender of dhann was tested in the most unglamorous way. Gurudev had taken to suggesting I fly from Mumbai to Gurgaon every month for bada guruvar — by air, not train. The difference in fare, to someone in financial difficulty at the time, was not small. I followed the instruction, grumbling inwardly, unable to understand the logic behind it. What arrived on the other side was not a lecture on the virtues of obedience. He gifted me some of the divine symbols he possessed.

*An earning for eternity. I felt foolish about my tight-fisted attitude.*

*(→ [[stories/hingori-dhann-airfare|Hingori's Airfare — Surrender of Dhann]])*

The episode that followed was instructive in a different register. Gurudev suggested my wife and I join him on a flight to Hyderabad for a wedding. All I had was INR 6,000 in cash, and I sent him a message asking to travel by train instead. I received no reply all day. The next morning, my wallet was missing. On approaching him to apologise, I saw a mischievous look in his eyes. He told me I should always do as asked, and not analyse his instructions. A few days later, the INR 6,000 turned up in the pocket of my blazer, at home, in Mumbai.

*Lost in Gurgaon and found in Mumbai!*

*(→ [[stories/hingori-wallet-lost-found|Lost in Gurgaon and Found in Mumbai — The Wallet]])*

Kabir understood what was operating on both sides of this exchange:

> *It is for the disciple to offer himself / Body and soul as a sacrifice at the Guru's feet; / But the true Master will not touch / A single penny that is offered to him.*
> — Kabir, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.9, pp.83–84

The return on surrender is always disproportionate. But it arrives only after you stop calculating.

**Where Faith and Surrender Meet**

To understand why surrender works, you have to understand what faith does. Faith, in this tradition, is not sentiment — it is a binding mechanism. *Guru Sutra* states it plainly: "A disciple's faith binds the guru to him." When the disciple's faith crosses a threshold of genuineness, the guru — in ways that cannot be verbally explained — registers this and feels compelled to deliver everything he can. Faith obligates the guru. Surrender is how the disciple enacts that faith in the world.

This is why the wallet story is not really a story about money. It is a story about what happens when faith falters: the moment Hingori questioned the instruction, the holding pattern of surrender was interrupted. When it was restored — through the apology, through the unconditional acceptance of the guru's direction — everything returned. The two are not sequential stages. They are, as *Guru Sutra* Ch.8 puts it, "the bridge that helps you cross over from your physical path to your spiritual future": faith is the structure of the bridge; surrender is the act of walking across it.

Guru Ram Dass once wrote: *"As soon as you give it all up, you can have it all. As long as you want power, you can't have it. The minute you don't want power, you'll have more than you ever dreamed possible."* This is not paradox for its own sake. It is a precise description of how the mechanism operates.

**The Surrender of Mind**

To surrender the body is to show up. To surrender wealth is to follow financial instructions without resistance. Both are difficult; neither is the hardest thing. The surrender of mann — mind and spirit — requires something altogether different: the willingness to accept your guru unconditionally, in full knowledge that his logic will often be opaque to you.

I found out what this actually meant one night, when a voice woke me — coming from within my own ear. It asked me whether I worshipped my guru because he was powerful, or because many admired him. I replied that I worshipped him for who he was and what he had done for me. The voice pressed: would I accept him even if he turned out not to be as great as I believed? Even if he had only tantric powers and had used them to impress me?

I did not know the answer immediately. But when it came, it was not contrived. I would accept him as my guru irrespective of how great he was. Even if he were not completely honest. I would surrender to him and accept him on an as-is-where-is basis.

*Yes. On an as-is-where-is basis.*

It was, I understood later, the voice of my own spirit speaking to me. When the conviction of that realisation settled, my relationship with Gurudev changed — from physical to metaphysical. The desperation to be near him, to manoeuvre my way into his room, to delay my departure — all of it receded. I found I could offer my salutations at his door without him even knowing I was there, and feel entirely satisfied. The mind, once surrendered at that depth, does not experience this as a loss. The grip loosens, and what you find underneath is not emptiness but a quieter kind of fullness.

*(→ [[stories/guru-sutra-ch4-inner-voice|The Inner Voice — Hingori's Stage 4 Awakening]])*

> *"There are things that your mind will never be able to understand or analyse. Always remember to do whatever you are asked without questioning it."*
> — Gurudev, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.9, p.88

William Booth once wrote: *"The greatness of a man's power is the measure of his surrender."* The disciple who grasps this discovers that the guru is not a crutch — he is a launch pad. The crutch holds you where you are. The launch pad requires you to stop holding on entirely, so that the velocity you are given can actually take effect.

Let go completely. The launch is the moment after.
