We were at one of Gurudev's biannual offsite seva camps — a cold evening at Mungaoli, and almost everyone around me was bundled in their cardigans. I slipped on my sweater before he arrived, not because I was cold, but because I didn't want him to think I was performing toughness. It was a small social vanity — the kind that floats just below the surface of awareness, barely worth naming. He noticed it instantly. Within minutes I was chattering and shivering, chills running down my spine, the cold now perfectly and unmistakably real. He had turned my pretence into reality as easily as one turns a page.

*(→ [[stories/mungaoli-sweater-incident|The Mungaoli Sweater]])*

Standing there, teeth rattling, I understood the lesson. Surrender to the mahaguru was not merely about grand gestures of renunciation. It included the sacrifice of small social performances — the everyday ego that dresses itself in consideration for others while really dressing itself. And as the evening continued, watching him sit unbothered in that same cold — the same camp, the same makeshift tent, the same open ground that surrounded everyone else — I saw something I had not yet named. He turned to me at some point and said it plainly, as though it had always been obvious: *"Grihasth mein bhi vairagya hai."* There is renunciation within the householder life too.

**What Vairagya Actually Is**

That evening at Mungaoli pointed at something larger than one shivering night. We tend to think of *vairagya* — detachment — as withdrawal. The renunciant who leaves his family, shaves his head, and retreats to a cave. But Gurudev never went to the cave. He held a government post at the IARI for decades, raised a family, ran a farm, counselled hundreds of disciples, and managed institutions. His life was lived completely in the world.

The detachment was internal. It was a shift in how he *inhabited* the world, not in how much of the world he touched. *Aatma Sutra* puts the requirement precisely:

> "If you aspire to higher levels of consciousness, then you need to detach not only from family, friends, and material objects but also from your own perceived identity."
> — *Aatma Sutra*, Ch.IX, p.101

That second clause is the harder one. Detaching from possessions is uncomfortable; detaching from the 'I' that claims to possess them is a different category of work altogether.

**The Paradox He Lived**

To understand what this looks like in an actual life, look at what Gurudev demonstrated every day. All of his followers believed he was closest to them. I did too. We felt he loved and cared for us far more than our parents ever did, and he spent more time with us than he did with his own children. It was only much later, when I got to know him better, that I realised he belonged to everyone, and yet to no one. He was ready to relieve our pain, but not feel it.

That phrase stayed with me for years: *ready to relieve our pain, but not feel it.* It is not coldness — a cold person withdraws and withholds. He did neither. He poured full attention into every disciple without keeping a private account of the exchange. No emotional ledger. And the reason every disciple felt uniquely cared for was precisely because he was owned by none of them: the love that asked nothing of him could be given to everyone without diminishing.

He said it himself once, in a way I have not found bettered:

> *"Main sab ka hoon aur sab mere hain.*
> *Lekin main kisi ka nahi hu aur mera koi nahi."*
>
> (I belong to all, and all belong to me.
> Yet I belong to no one, and no one belongs to me.)
> — Gurudev, *The Guru of Gurus*, p.108

The two lines are not a contradiction. They are the same state described from opposite directions. The first is what his disciples saw; the second is what made it possible.

**To Conquer Sorrow, Conquer Happiness First**

Understanding this paradox is one thing; arriving at it is another. *Aatma Sutra* offers the seesaw as a way to see the mechanics. Happiness and sorrow sit at opposite ends of the same plank. When you climb toward one end, the other swings up with equal force — the structure guarantees it. The way off the seesaw is not to hold the joyful end down. The way off is the fulcrum: the still centre where neither end dominates, the state of "happylessness" — not the absence of feeling, but the absence of the plank's pull.

In my early years as a disciple, Gurudev gave me an instruction I did not at first understand. He said: if you wish to conquer sorrow, you must first conquer happiness. For four years I practised no laughter, no joy, no enjoyment at parties or at home, and no reaction to good news of any kind. I walked around confused, not knowing how to get a grip on life. He had begun to use his eraser, and the process of unlearning had started.

*(→ [[stories/hingori-mind-training-non-duality|Four Years of Destroying the Mind]])*

It was, I can say now, profoundly boring. But it was a boredom of equanimity — a quiet that held no sharp edges. Over years, my passion turned into dispassion, and several likes softened alongside their corresponding dislikes. The seesaw slowed. He had not removed my emotions; he had removed my investment in them. Which is, I now understand, the precise goal:

> "Practising detachment from the outcome of a deed helped make seva a habit. That meant there was no joy to be found in it and no pain to be felt doing it. It was a duty that needed to be carried out without emotion and with utmost humility."
> — Hingori, *The Guru of Gurus*, p.108

This is where *vairagya* actually arrives: not in the dramatic exit from the world, but in the quiet discovery that you can act inside it — fully, generously — without the outcome owning you.

**A Direction, Not a Destination**

I want to be honest about where this leads. I have not given it all up yet. I accept that, and I am satisfied with the results so far. *Vairagya* is not a switch you throw; it is a direction you walk in. The question is not "have I arrived?" but "am I facing the right way?"

The smallest move counts. Wearing a sweater out of social vanity rather than cold is a small move in the wrong direction. Noticing it, being shown it, and choosing not to repeat it — that is a small move in the right one.

*Begin there.*
