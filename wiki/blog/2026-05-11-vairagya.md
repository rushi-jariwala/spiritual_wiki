The camp had been set up in the open, and the morning air at the offsite was sharp enough to make teeth chatter. When Gurudev arrived, I had already pulled on a sweater — not because the cold was unbearable, but because I did not want him to think I was pretending it wasn't. An odd logic, I know: the social performance of not performing. Gurudev read it in a second. Within minutes I was shivering inside that sweater more intensely than anyone in the camp. He had made the cold real, as if turning a dial in nature itself, to demonstrate what surrender actually includes — the sacrifice of false ego, the social graces we stage for others, the make-believe that never deceives anyone but ourselves.

When I eventually stopped shaking, he said, quietly: *"Grihasth mein bhi vairagya hai."* There is renunciation within the householder life too.

**What Detachment Actually Means**

That sentence planted itself and took years to germinate, because I had assumed, like most people, that *vairagya* meant withdrawal — the ochre robe, the cave, the studied indifference of a man who has left. Gurudev demolished that picture through the simple fact of his own life. He was a husband, a father, an employer, and the centre of every room he entered. His disciples competed for his attention. Every one of us was certain, for a time, that he loved us more than the others. The truth, as I came to understand it, was both more straightforward and more astounding:

> "Gurudev role-played affection to perfection. He manipulated us into believing he loved us more than the others. The truth is he loved everyone equally but without an iota of emotion."
> — Hingori, *The Guru of Gurus*, p.108

This is not indifference masquerading as warmth. It is love operating as a function rather than a feeling — love that gives without requiring the giver to be moved by it. He was always ready to relieve our pain. He simply did not feel it himself. In his own words, the paradox had an exact form:

> *"Main sab ka hoon aur sab mere hain.*
> *Lekin main kisi ka nahi hu aur mera koi nahi."*

I belong to all, and all belong to me. Yet I belong to no one, and no one belongs to me.

Because he belonged to no one in particular, he could belong to everyone without remainder. That is the precise geometry of *vairagya* at its fullest expression.

**The Seesaw and the Still Centre**

To understand why this is not coldness, consider the image of a seesaw. *"It is only at the centre of a seesaw that emotions are at equilibrium. There is no joy at the centre, but then there is also no sadness."* We spend our lives at the ends of it — joy on one side, sorrow on the other — imagining that the goal is to stay as long as possible on the joy end. *Vairagya* is the recognition that the real destination is the fulcrum: not the suppression of feeling, but the natural settling that arrives when the 'I' is no longer claiming authorship over every event that passes through it.

Gurudev did not reach this place by becoming numb. He reached it through sustained practice, until seva itself became habit, and habit gradually cleared the emotional ledger:

> "Practising detachment from the outcome of a deed helped make seva a habit. That meant there was no joy to be found in it and no pain to be felt doing it. It was a duty that needed to be carried out without emotion and with utmost humility."
> — Hingori, *The Guru of Gurus*, p.108

**The Kitchen in Mussoorie**

The same principle was visible even in the smallest domestic scenes. On a trip to Mussoorie, Indu ji had cooked for the whole party — a potato and capsicum dish with rotis — and made sure everyone had eaten before attending to herself. When she finally looked into the kadai, only a small portion remained. Gurudev walked into the kitchen uninvited, insisted on being served there and then, and when the modest helping appeared, divided it in two and handed her a plate. He ate his half with a smile. When he finished, he turned to Renu ji and said: *"Putt, jennu khellaan che mazaa agaya na, khaane da mazaa nahin rehnda."*

One who learns the pleasure of feeding others, seldom cares about feeding oneself.

*(→ [[stories/gurudev-indu-subzi-mussoorie|Gurudev, Indu ji, and the Last Portion of Subzi]])*

There was nothing dramatic in the scene — no discourse on renunciation, no pointed teaching. Only a small kitchen, a divided plate, and a man demonstrating in two minutes what most of us spend lifetimes trying to describe.

**How to Begin**

*Vairagya* is not a project that requires you to uproot your loves or abandon your responsibilities. The tradition does not ask for that. It asks for a shift in the way you inhabit your life — with full engagement, but without the emotional ownership that converts roles into identities and bonds into chains. *Aatma Sutra* is direct on this:

> "If you aspire to higher levels of consciousness, then you need to detach not only from family, friends, and material objects but also from your own perceived identity."
> — Hingori, *Aatma Sutra*, Ch.IX, p.101

That last phrase is the harder part. Detachment from possessions is conceivable; detachment from the self-image — the story we tell ourselves about who we are and who needs us — is another matter. And yet that is where the real movement begins.

Start there, and let the rest follow.
