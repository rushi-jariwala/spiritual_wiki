I was at one of Gurudev's offsite camps in Mungaoli — one of those biannual retreats where the seva was demanding and the comforts were nonexistent. He and his team had pitched tents on open ground. The nights were cold. Almost everyone around me had pulled on a cardigan or a shawl. I wore a sweater too, but not because I needed it. I wore it so that Gurudev would not think I was making a show of being unaffected by the cold — performing hardiness for his approval. A small vanity. A social calculation.

He saw through it in an instant. Without a word, he made the cold real. I began to chatter and shiver, *chills down my spine*, while still wearing the sweater that was meant to spare me from looking ridiculous. He had turned my pretence into physical reality — and in doing so, demonstrated his control not only over my mind and body but over nature itself. I soon realised that surrender to the mahaguru included the surrender of false ego, social graces, and make-believe. He walked away without comment. But later that evening, I heard him say what would become one of the most significant seeds he ever planted in me: *"Grihasth mein bhi vairagya hai."* There is renunciation within the householder life too.

**What Vairagya Asks of You**

This is where many people misread detachment. They hear the word *vairagya* and picture a man in an orange robe, cold-eyed and withdrawn, who has left his family behind for a cave. That is not what Gurudev taught. *Aatma Sutra* puts it plainly:

> "If you aspire to higher levels of consciousness, then you need to detach not only from family, friends, and material objects but also from your own perceived identity."

The outer layer — loosening your grip on pleasure and pain, on possessions and approval — is what most people understand as detachment. But *vairagya* has a second, harder layer: detachment from the story you carry about who you are. The sweater at Mungaoli was not about fabric or cold. It was about the self-image that had to be managed and protected every hour of the day, the small performances through which the ego maintains its shape. That was what Gurudev reached into and dissolved.

**The Paradox at the Centre**

To understand this more clearly, watch what full vairagya looks like from the inside — not as an abstraction, but as a lived reality I observed over decades.

All of Gurudev's followers believed he was closest to them. I did too. I felt he loved and cared for me far more than my own parents. He spent more time with his disciples than he did with his own children. Much later, when I knew him better, I understood: he belonged to everyone, and yet to no one. *He was ready to relieve our pain, but not feel it.* The love was entirely real in its effect — each of us felt uniquely cared for — but there was no emotional ownership underneath it. Because he had no private stake in any of us, he could be fully present for all of us. The love that asked nothing of him could be given to everyone without ever running short.

He said it himself once, in a single pair of lines:

*"Main sab ka hoon aur sab mere hain.*
*Lekin main kisi ka nahi hu aur mera koi nahi."*

*(I belong to all, and all belong to me.*
*Yet I belong to no one, and no one belongs to me.)*

The two lines are not contradictions. They describe the same state from opposite ends. Unlimited belonging and complete non-attachment are not opposites — they are the same realisation. His love was not a rationed resource. It was unlimited precisely because there was no ego at stake in the giving.

Think of what happens without this balance. Marlon Brando immersed himself so completely in Don Corleone that he needed six months of psychiatric care to exit the role. Rod Steiger played Napoleon and the character swallowed him whole — he too needed clinical help to find his way back. The role was meant to be a vehicle; it became a cage. In life, we do exactly this with every role we inhabit — parent, spouse, colleague, devotee — when we mistake the performance for our identity. *Vairagya* is the practice that keeps you in the role without being imprisoned by it.

**The Training Ground**

Recognising this intellectually is one thing. Being trained into it is entirely another, and I speak from four years of learning it in the most uncomfortable way I can imagine.

Gurudev's opening act with me was to destroy my mind — figuratively speaking. Everything I had relied upon to understand myself and the world stopped working. I walked around confused, not knowing how to think. He had begun to use his eraser, and the process of unlearning had started.

The first instruction he gave me was this: if you desire to conquer sorrow, you must first conquer happiness. For four years, I practised no laughter, no joy, no reaction to good news at home, at work, or at parties. It was almost like deadening emotions to attain boredom. A tedious programme by any measure. But on the other side of the boredom was something I had not previously encountered: equanimity of mind. My passion turned to dispassion. Strong likes became mild preferences. Old aversions softened. Without quite knowing it, I was being led toward non-duality — *"slowly lifting the veil of maya,"* as I understood it only much later.

*(→ [[stories/hingori-mind-training-non-duality|Four Years of Destroying the Mind]])*

By the time this training settled in the body, I understood what it had actually removed: not love, not engagement, not care — but the emotional ownership of outcomes. Seva became a habit precisely because there was no joy to be found in it and no pain to be felt doing it. It was a duty, carried out without emotion and with humility. What looks from the outside like coldness is, on the inside, something more like steadiness: the difference between inhabiting a play fully and living trapped inside one.

Vairagya does not ask you to feel less. It asks you to stop performing your feelings — and then, quietly, to stop claiming ownership of them too.
