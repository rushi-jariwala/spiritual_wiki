One winter, at one of Gurudev's biannual offsite camps in Mungaoli, I made a small and telling mistake. The evening had turned cold, and almost everyone around me had wrapped themselves in cardigans and shawls. I — not wanting Gurudev to think I was the kind who made a show of toughness — put on a sweater upon his arrival. What I had not counted on was that the pretence itself would be read.

*"He made me chatter and shiver with chills down my spine because I wore a sweater upon his arrival. I didn't want him to think I was bragging about not being cold when almost everyone else was snug in their cardigans. By turning my pretence into reality, he demonstrated his control not only over my mind and body but also over nature."*

He had exposed the small performance — the social grace dressed up as modesty — and collapsed it by making it real. *(→ [[stories/mungaoli-sweater-incident|The Mungaoli Sweater]])* I soon understood what the exercise meant: surrender to the mahaguru includes surrendering false ego, social graces, and make-believe. That day he offered four quiet words that I have not forgotten: *"Grihasth mein bhi vairagya hai."* There is detachment even within the householder's life.

**What Detachment Is Not**

That moment at Mungaoli gave me my first embodied understanding of vairagya — though the concept is often misread, even by sincere seekers. People hear "detachment" and picture withdrawal: from family, from work, from laughter, from the world. That is not what is being asked for. *Aatma Sutra* puts it plainly:

> "If you aspire to higher levels of consciousness, then you need to detach not only from family, friends, and material objects but also from your own perceived identity."

The sequence matters. External detachment — from desires, pleasures, commercial ambitions — is the first layer. The harder and deeper work is detachment from the 'I' that believes it is doing the detaching. That is vairagya in its fullness. *(→ [[concepts/vairagya]])*

Think of a seesaw. Joy is at one end; sorrow is at the other. Most of us spend our lives lunging from one side to the other, calling the upswing "a good day" and the downswing "a bad one." The goal of detachment is not to disable the seesaw — it is to find the fulcrum: the still point at the centre where bliss is possible because it is not tied to either end. As the teaching puts it: if you want to reduce sorrow, you must first reduce joy. That is not pessimism. That is the physics of the inner life.

**The Four Years**

To understand this more clearly, let me tell you what it actually cost me to learn it.

In the first four years as Gurudev's potential disciple, he used his eraser. Not a gentle one. My style of thinking, my attitudes, my certainties — all of it was quietly dismantled until I walked around confused, unable to get a grip on my own life. Then came the instruction:

*"The first thing he taught me was, if I desired to conquer sorrow, I had to conquer happiness. For four long years, I practised no laughter, no joy, no enjoyment at home, work or parties, and had no reaction to good news of any kind. It was almost like deadening emotions to attain boredom."* *(→ [[stories/hingori-mind-training-non-duality|Four Years of Destroying the Mind]])*

What emerged on the other side was not despondency. It was equanimity. My passion turned into dispassion. Likes and attachments reduced; dislikes and aversions along with them. He had led me, step by careful step, in the direction of non-duality — slowly lifting the veil of maya. I will admit that even now, decades later, I have not been able to give it all up. But I accept that, and am satisfied with the results.

**The Living Proof**

Understanding vairagya is one thing; seeing it embodied in someone is another entirely.

All of Gurudev's followers believed they were his closest ones. I did too. We felt he loved and cared for each of us more than our parents ever had. He spent more time with us than he did with his own children. And yet, when I knew him well enough, I realised the truth of it. He belonged to everyone and to no one — simultaneously. He expressed this directly, in the way he spoke:

> "Main sab ka hoon aur sab mere hain.
> Lekin main kisi ka nahi hu aur mera koi nahi."
> (I belong to all, and all belong to me.
> Yet I belong to no one, and no one belongs to me.)
> — Gurudev, *The Guru of Gurus*, p.108

This is not a riddle. It is the most precise description of what vairagya looks like when it is complete. Because he belonged to no one in particular, he could belong to everyone equally. He was ready to relieve our pain — but not feel it. Practising detachment from the outcome of a deed had made seva a habit; there was no joy to be found in it and no pain to be felt doing it. Love as function, not as feeling. Presence without possession.

That is the paradox at the heart of this teaching: the one who holds nothing is the one who can give everything.

Start there.
