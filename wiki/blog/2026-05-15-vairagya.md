I remember the evening at the camp in Mungaoli. Everyone else in the group had arrived bundled in warm woolens, and when Gurudev walked in, there I was in my sweater. The truth was, I had not put it on because I was cold. I put it on because I did not want him to think I was showing off by going without one — that small, respectable social performance we all stage unconsciously, dozens of times a day. He noticed.

Within minutes, I was chattering with chills down my spine. He had made my pretence real — his control extended not only over my mind but over my body and, apparently, over nature itself. The teaching arrived without a word of explanation: *"Surrender to the mahaguru included the sacrifice of false ego, social graces, and make-believe."* And that evening, walking through the decrepit guest house without a trace of concern for the poor conditions, he said something I have carried ever since: *"Grihasth mein bhi vairagya hai."* There is renunciation within the householder life too.

**The Fulcrum, Not the End**

That sweater was a small lesson in a larger principle — one I would spend years trying to understand. *Vairagya* is the Sanskrit word for detachment, and when most people hear it, they imagine a monk walking away from the world. What it actually asks is more subtle: not that you leave your relationships and obligations, but that you shift how you stand inside them.

*Aatma Sutra* puts the aspiration precisely: *"If you aspire to higher levels of consciousness, then you need to detach not only from family, friends, and material objects but also from your own perceived identity."* That last phrase is the harder half. The world of objects is the outer layer of attachment; the self — the personality, the self-image, the habits of feeling we have assembled and come to call 'I' — is the deeper layer underneath.

Think of a seesaw. At the centre, neither end is in the air. That still point — not joy and not sorrow, but the fulcrum between them — is what this practice points toward. *"It is only at the centre of a seesaw that emotions are at equilibrium. There is no joy at the centre, but then there is also no sadness. If you want to reduce sorrow, you must first reduce joy."* This is not a prescription for misery. It is a prescription for stability. The seesaw only breaks you when you insist on riding the ends.

**Conquering Sorrow by Conquering Joy**

To understand what this practice actually requires, I need to tell you about my first four years as a disciple. The first instruction Gurudev gave me — not a mantra, not a ritual, but the very first teaching he gave — was this: *"If I desired to conquer sorrow, I had to conquer happiness."*

For four years I practised no laughter, no joy, no enjoyment at home or at work or at parties, and no reaction to good news of any kind. I walked around confused, feeling I had lost my grip on life entirely. All I had ever learnt seemed irrelevant. He had taken an eraser to my mind and the process of unlearning had begun. I did not know what to think.

But what he was creating in me — slowly, through that long period of intentional boredom — was equanimity: a mind no longer held hostage by the next swing of the pendulum. My passion turned into dispassion. My likes and my aversions both reduced. I was being inducted, as I came to understand much later, into non-duality. *"Do not mistake this to be a story of despondency or loss. No. It is my story of slowly lifting the veil of maya, and being inducted into non-duality."*

To enjoy something is to sit at the zenith of an emotion that will, by the logic of the seesaw, pull you down to the nadir later. Not because life is cruel, but because that is what an unexhausted samskar does — it fructifies, peaks, and then presents its bill. *Vairagya* is the practice of stepping off before the bill arrives.

**He Belonged to No One**

All of that — the four years of erasing the mind, the slow loosening of attachment to my own feelings — was, I came to understand, preparation for witnessing what *vairagya* looks like when it is fully realised. Gurudev himself was the living example.

Every one of his followers believed he was closest to them. I did too. We felt he loved and cared for us more than our parents ever had, and he spent more time with us than with his own children. *"Much later, when I got to know him better, I realised that he belonged to everyone, and yet, to no one. He was ready to relieve our pain, but not feel it."* That one sentence — ready to relieve our pain, but not feel it — is the operative distinction between *vairagya* and coldness.

He once put his own state in two lines. The first: *"Main sab ka hoon aur sab mere hain"* — I belong to all, and all belong to me. The second: *"Lekin main kisi ka nahi hu aur mera koi nahi"* — Yet I belong to no one, and no one belongs to me. These two lines are not a contradiction. They are the same truth described from opposite directions. Because he belonged to no one in particular, his attention was never pulled away from anyone. He role-played affection to perfection, making each of us feel uniquely cared for. The love was real in its effect. The truth was that he loved everyone equally, but without an iota of emotion — fully present and entirely free.

When seva had become his way of life, there was no joy in it and no pain. Not because he suppressed those feelings, but because the 'I' that would have felt them had quietly stepped aside. Practising detachment from the outcome of a deed had made seva a habit — a duty carried out without emotion and with utmost humility.

The sweater in Mungaoli, the four years of erasing the mind, the two-line formula — all three point at the same understanding from a different angle. The self that does not cling to what it likes or resist what it dislikes is not a diminished self. It is the self that has found the still point.

Step off the end. Find the centre.
