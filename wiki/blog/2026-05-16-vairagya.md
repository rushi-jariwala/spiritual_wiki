One cold morning at an offsite camp in Mungaoli, I made a small social calculation that cost me a great deal of warmth. Everyone around me had pulled on cardigans and woollens against the January chill. I had not. Not because I was genuinely warm — I was freezing — but because I did not want Gurudev to think I was showing off by being the only person who had needed a sweater.

He looked at me once. Within moments, the cold became real. Not the polite chill of an early morning, but the bone-deep shiver of a man whose pretence had just been turned into actuality. *I soon realised that surrender to the mahaguru included the sacrifice of false ego, social graces, and make-believe.*

*(→ [[stories/mungaoli-sweater-incident|The Mungaoli Sweater]])*

He said nothing elaborate. His words, when they came, were few and precise: *"Grihasth mein bhi vairagya hai."* There is renunciation within the householder life too.

That sentence germinated in me for years.

**What Vairagya Is Not**

To understand what those words actually asked of me, I had to clear away the most common misunderstanding. Most people hear *vairagya* and picture a renunciate — someone who has walked away from family, possessions, and the world. What Gurudev demonstrated was something different, and in certain ways harder. He lived in bachelorhood rooms of a hundred and twenty square feet, slept in decrepit district guest houses, pitched tents on open grounds when the schedule demanded it. And yet he was a householder, a family man, a working professional. He was not outside the world. He was entirely inside it, and somehow unentangled by it.

Mundane aspects of life neither perturbed nor distracted him. He was away from his family for extended periods, yet no one who knew him would have called him neglectful — because the care he carried for those around him was genuine, attentive, and constant. He had simply arranged something inside himself that kept that care from hardening into dependency, his or theirs.

The householder stays. The renunciate within the householder simply does not cling.

**The Two Detachments**

What exactly is being let go of? *Aatma Sutra* is precise:

> "If you aspire to higher levels of consciousness, then you need to detach not only from family, friends, and material objects but also from your own perceived identity."
> — *Aatma Sutra*, Ch.IX, p.101

The first detachment — from external bonds, from pleasures and possessions — is the one most seekers begin with. It is difficult enough. The second detachment — from the perceived identity, the self-image one has spent decades constructing and maintaining — is the deeper work and the one that cannot be rushed or faked. It is also where most sincere practitioners discover the real resistance, because by then it is not the world they are trying to release. It is themselves.

The small social performance I was running in Mungaoli that morning — wearing a sweater without feeling the cold, managing impressions, trying to appear a certain way — was not vanity in the ordinary sense. It was the perceived identity doing what it does: arranging the self to be seen in a flattering light. Gurudev dissolved it in sixty seconds.

**The Paradox of Full Presence**

All of Gurudev's followers believed he was closest to them. I did too. We felt he loved and cared for us far more than our parents ever had, spent more time with us than with his own children. Much later, when I knew him better, I found the truth underneath that feeling: he belonged to everyone, and yet to no one. He was ready to relieve our pain, but not feel it.

His own words said it with a precision I could not improve on:

> *"Main sab ka hoon aur sab mere hain.*
> *Lekin main kisi ka nahi hu aur mera koi nahi."*
> (I belong to all, and all belong to me.
> Yet I belong to no one, and no one belongs to me.)
> — Gurudev, *The Guru of Gurus*, p.108

This is *vairagya* at its peak — not the erasure of love, but love without ownership. The love was real in its effect; disciples genuinely felt uniquely cared for. Yet there was no emotional possession underneath it. Because he was attached to none of us in particular, he could give himself to all of us completely. The love that asked nothing of him could reach everyone without diminishing.

**The Training No One Tells You About**

Understanding this with the intellect is one thing. Living through its training is another matter entirely.

In the first four years of my discipleship, Gurudev's method was not instruction — it was demolition. He used his eraser, as I have come to think of it, and the process of unlearning began. I lost complete faith in my style of thinking, my attitudes, my ways of responding to almost everything I had known. I did not know what to think.

Then came the specific discipline: for four years, I practised no laughter, no joy, no enjoyment — at home, at work, at parties. No reaction to good news of any kind. *It was almost like deadening emotions to attain boredom.*

*(→ [[stories/hingori-mind-training-non-duality|Four Years of Destroying the Mind]])*

That boredom, as it turned out, was a state of equanimity. He had led me to a place that had no room for the ordinary swings. Raga and dwesa — attraction and aversion — are the two ends of the same plank. Swing toward one and you guarantee an equal movement toward the other. What he was building in me, through those years of mandated quiet, was the capacity to hold the fulcrum: not joy, not sorrow, but the still centre where neither end dominates. To reduce sorrow, I first had to reduce joy.

My passion turned into dispassion. Likes diminished; dislikes diminished along with them. Do not mistake this for a story of despondency. It is precisely the opposite — it is the story of slowly lifting the veil of maya, and beginning, however tentatively, the journey into non-duality.

**The Gift on the Other Side**

On the other side of those years, I understood what Gurudev had been pointing at since Mungaoli. Practising detachment from the outcome of a deed made seva a habit. That meant there was no joy to be found in it and no pain to be felt doing it. It became a duty carried out without emotion, with as much humility as I could muster. And in that absence of emotional ownership, something opened — availability, the capacity to be present for many without being depleted by any one.

That is the gift of *vairagya*: not less feeling, but feeling that is no longer enslaved to its own swings.

The sweater in Mungaoli was a small pretence. What Gurudev dissolved that morning was something larger: the habit of performing for others, of managing impressions, of being attached to how one appears. The cold he sent through me did its work.

Stop performing the cold.
