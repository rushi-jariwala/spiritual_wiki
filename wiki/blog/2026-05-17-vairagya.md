One winter morning at an offsite camp in Mungaoli, I made a small social calculation. Almost everyone around me was in a cardigan or woolly jacket, snug against the cold. I was wearing a sweater too — but not because I was cold. I wore it because I did not want Gurudev to think I was showing off by going without one. A small vanity, dressed up as consideration for others.

He saw through it within minutes. I began to shiver. The cold entered my sweater as though the wool were gauze. He had made my pretence real — turned my performed modesty into genuine discomfort — demonstrating, without a word, his control over my mind, body, and nature itself. I sat there chattering and chilled in the sweater I had worn as camouflage.

*"Grihasth mein bhi vairagya hai,"* he said. Even the householder can renounce.

*(→ [[stories/mungaoli-sweater-incident|The Mungaoli Sweater]])*

That morning taught me something no lecture could have. Surrender to the guru includes the surrender of false ego, social graces, and the small performances we stage for others. And *vairagya* — detachment — is not the property of the monk alone. It lives inside ordinary life, woven between daily obligations and relationships, invisible to those who don't yet know where to look.

**The Man Who Belonged to No One**

That teaching lives most vividly in the person of Gurudev himself. I have spent decades close to him, and I can tell you that every one of his followers believed he was closest to them. I believed it too. We felt he loved and cared for us more than our own parents ever did, and he spent more time with us than with his own children. It was only later — much later — that I understood the truth.

He belonged to everyone, and yet to no one.

He said it himself, plainly:

> *"Main sab ka hoon aur sab mere hain.*
> *Lekin main kisi ka nahi hu aur mera koi nahi."*
> (I belong to all, and all belong to me.
> Yet I belong to no one, and no one belongs to me.)
> — Gurudev, *The Guru of Gurus*, p.108

The paradox is not a contradiction. It is the same state seen from opposite ends. Because he was attached to no one in particular, he could belong to everyone equally. There was no queue for his care. It never ran short, because it was not the kind that depletes when shared. He role-played affection to perfection, making each of us feel we were the most loved — and yet he loved everyone equally, without an iota of emotion. Love as function, not as feeling. He was ready to relieve our pain, but not to feel it.

This is what *vairagya* looks like at full integration: the love is entirely real in its effect; what is absent is the emotional ownership underneath it.

**The Seesaw Nobody Warned Me About**

What made that kind of love possible is a principle that demands something counterintuitive from us. *Vairagya* asks us to reduce joy in order to reduce sorrow — because the two sit at opposite ends of the same seesaw, and you cannot hold one end still without the other rising.

> "It is only at the centre of a seesaw that emotions are at equilibrium. There is no joy at the centre, but then there is also no sadness. If you want to reduce sorrow, you must first reduce joy."

Bliss is not happiness. Happiness is the upswing of the seesaw; bliss is the fulcrum — the stillness at the centre, where neither end dominates. What most people pursue as spiritual peace is often just the happiness end of the seesaw, not yet aware of how fast the sorrow end is returning. Detachment is not the extinguishing of feeling; it is the discovery of a position from which neither end can drag you down.

Gurudev's teaching on this was not theoretical. He put me through it.

**Four Years Without Joy**

In the first four years of my discipleship, Gurudev's opening move was to destroy my mind — figuratively speaking. I lost complete faith in my style of thinking, in my attitude towards almost everything. All I had ever learned seemed irrelevant. I walked around confused, not knowing how to get a grip on my life.

The first thing he taught me was this: if you desire to conquer sorrow, you must first conquer happiness. For four long years, I practised no laughter, no joy, no enjoyment — at home, at work, at parties — no reaction to good news of any kind.

*"Though this state of boredom was very boring, it was one of equanimity of the mind."*

*(→ [[stories/hingori-mind-training-non-duality|Four Years of Destroying the Mind]])*

He led me, through that long emptying, toward non-duality. My passion turned into dispassion. Likes and attachments fell away — so did dislikes and aversions. Do not mistake this for despondency or loss. It was, slowly and unmistakably, the lifting of the veil of maya.

*Aatma Sutra* makes the aspiration explicit:

> "If you aspire to higher levels of consciousness, then you need to detach not only from family, friends, and material objects but also from your own perceived identity."
> — *Aatma Sutra*, Ch.IX, p.101

I will tell you honestly: I have not given everything up yet. Some desires for touch and taste remain. I accept that, and I am satisfied with what the practice has yielded. *Vairagya* is a direction, not a destination. You do not arrive; you travel.

Over time, practising detachment from the outcome of a deed made seva a habit. There was no joy to be found in it and no pain to be felt doing it. It became a duty carried out without emotion and with utmost humility. That is where the practice finally lands: not the suppression of joy and pain, but their natural subsidence when the 'I' stops claiming authorship over the act.

**The Sweater Was Never the Problem**

Both stories — the sweater and the four years — point at the same truth. Attachment does not always look like passion for wealth or fame. Often it arrives in small performances: the sweater worn so no one thinks ill of us, the laughter offered so no one feels avoided, the grief extended beyond its natural span so it can pass for love. These are attachments to the perceived self — to the image we maintain for others and, far more stubbornly, for ourselves.

Gurudev dismantled mine, one shiver at a time.

*Vairagya* does not ask us to abandon the world or drain the warmth from our relationships. Relationships are inhabited fully — they are inhabited as roles, not as identities. The love remains real in its effect. What is given up is only the emotional ownership underneath it.

Wear the role. Do not become it.
