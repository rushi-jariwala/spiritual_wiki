My arthritis had returned, and I was waiting in the queue at the sthan for my second meeting with Gurudev. Puranji, one of the older disciples, pulled me aside and asked what I planned to ask for when my turn came. I admitted I did not know. He suggested I ask for guru kripa.

I did not truly understand what those two words meant. But they sounded right — poetic, even — so when I finally stepped forward and spoke them, I held to them as though I did.

What followed was something I had not anticipated. Gurudev stopped the queue, took me to a separate room, and insisted, with some persistence, that I ask for what I actually wanted. I repeated the phrase again, like a parrot repeating words it cannot parse. It turned into a battle of wits — and a strange one at that. I simply could not understand why he wanted me to choose something more concrete. Eventually, he grinned and appeared to relent. He patted me on the back and said, *"You will get a lot more than you can imagine."*

Those were kind words. Walking out, however, I was not entirely impressed with myself. My arthritis was still present. All I seemed to have managed was an exchange of two words I could not define for two more words I could not yet verify. It felt like a poor trade. For a while, I thought it was.

I was more wrong about that assessment than I have been about almost anything else.

**What Guru Kripa Actually Is**

To understand why those two words turned out to be the best possible ask, it is worth being clear about what guru kripa is not. It is not the warmth a guru shows after a good seva. It is not the pleasure of being approved of, the reassurance of a smile, or the social comfort of being known to be in the guru's good books. These things are pleasant, but they do not add up to grace.

*Guru Sutra* is plain about this: *"Sometimes, a reprimanding sentence from the guru can mean more than a profusion of kind words. Thus, guru kripa may not always be sweet and sugarcoated but can be astringent, like a bitter pill that cures."*

The disciple being corrected, tested, or made uncomfortable by the guru may be receiving more grace in that moment than the one being praised. The bitter pill is still medicine — the cure is the point, not the taste. Over years in discipleship, I came to recognise this from the inside: many of the moments that felt like setbacks were the ones doing the deepest work.

Guru kripa also operates at a level most people do not suspect. It does not address only the outer personality — the surface of health, behaviour, or circumstance. For grace to be genuinely transmitted, it must work through all three minds: physical, mental, and spiritual, corresponding to the three bodies we each carry. Being gracious in manner, courteous in speech, generous in the guru's presence — these may earn social favour. Guru kripa is a different category altogether.

**The Coincidence That Was Not Random**

The invisible operation of grace — its workings through circumstance rather than through drama — is easier to understand in a story.

There was a disciple named Karmakarji, from the Kolkata sthan, who worked in a pharmaceutical company where cocktail parties for executives were a professional obligation. He held a personal practice of not consuming alcohol on Thursdays. One Thursday, a company event was scheduled. He attended; he broke the restriction. Shaken, he went to his own guru — Dutta Babu, a colleague of Gurudev's — and sought forgiveness.

Dutta Babu did not merely absolve him. He told Karmakarji not to be discouraged, but to use the power of intent: to will it, with sincerity, so that his company would never again hold a cocktail party on a Thursday for as long as he was employed there. Karmakarji willed it.

His company never held a cocktail party on a Thursday again, for as long as he worked there.

A coincidence? Perhaps. But *Guru Sutra* notes, quietly, that guru kripa at work here is a distinct possibility. This is how grace typically moves — not as a visible, announced event, but as a quiet adjustment in the texture of a life, indistinguishable from ordinary luck unless you know what you are looking at.

**What Carries Across**

There is one more dimension of guru kripa that took me the longest time to grasp, and it may be the most significant of all.

*"A guru's grace on a person is not merely physical in nature but one that might be in continuity from previous lives and may continue to exist till the guru can guide the disciple towards the attainment of mukti."*

This changes the entire frame. The grace accumulated in one lifetime does not reset with the body. It continues — crossing incarnations, working through the kaarna sharir, the causal body that is the only inheritance the soul truly carries — toward the horizon of liberation. A guru's care for a disciple is not the project of a single life. It is a continuity of attention spanning as many births as the journey requires.

That is why, looking back at that moment in the queue — Puranji's advice still ringing in my ears, the arthritis still in my joints — I can say without any doubt that asking for guru kripa was the most consequential choice of my discipleship. Everything that followed: the capacity to serve, the spiritual wealth I count as my truest richness, the ability to be of use to life in its many forms — all of it traces back to those two words spoken with sincerity but without comprehension.

> He fills the disciple with wisdom
>      and sets his soul aglow
>  so that one day the disciple can
> re-ignite the flame in many more.
> — *Guru Sutra*, Ch.12, p.126

Do not wait until you understand what you are asking for. Ask for the grace.
