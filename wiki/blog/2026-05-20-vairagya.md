One winter, at one of Gurudev's biannual offsite seva camps, I wore a sweater.

Almost everyone else had wrapped themselves in cardigans and woollens against the Mungaoli cold. I had come without one — it was genuinely manageable — but when I saw the rest of the group bundled in layers, I didn't want Gurudev to think I was making a point of it. So I pulled on the sweater. Not because I was cold. Because I did not want to *appear* to be showing off by not being cold.

*He made me chatter and shiver with chills down my spine.* Gurudev read the social calculation inside that small act and made the cold real. I sat there shivering inside the garment I had worn for all the wrong reasons. No commentary was needed. I soon understood that surrender to the mahaguru included the sacrifice of false ego, social graces, and make-believe — not just the large pretences, but the small ones that prop up the daily self.

At the end of that camp, he said something I have carried ever since: *"Grihasth mein bhi vairagya hai."* There is renunciation within the householder life too.

**The Grip Beneath the Surface**

The sweater incident opened something I would spend years working through. *Vairagya* is commonly taught as withdrawal — the sadhu relinquishing property and family for the interior life. Gurudev's version is more demanding and, for most of us, far more relevant. *Aatma Sutra* states both layers plainly:

> "If you aspire to higher levels of consciousness, then you need to detach not only from family, friends, and material objects but also from your own perceived identity."
> — *Aatma Sutra*, Ch.IX, p.101

The first layer — loosening the grip on people and things — is hard enough. Most sincere seekers can do it, given time and goodwill. The second layer, detachment from the perceived *self*, is where things get genuinely difficult. The sweater was not about cloth. It was about the performance of identity — the 'I' that calculated other people's impressions before acting. That is the deeper grip, and it is the subtler target of *vairagya*. *(→ [[concepts/vairagya]])*

**Learning the Seesaw**

To understand what he was actually asking of us, you need to know what Gurudev set in motion at the beginning of my years as a potential disciple. His opening act was to use his eraser — to dismantle whatever confidence I had in my own thinking, until almost everything I had previously held as certain seemed irrelevant. I walked around confused, not knowing how to get a grip on my life.

Then came the instruction: *"If you desire to conquer sorrow, you must first conquer happiness."*

For four years I practiced this literally — no laughter, no enjoyment at home or at parties, no visible reaction to good news of any kind. *It was almost like deadening emotions to attain boredom.* The boredom was genuine. But underneath it, something else arrived: an equanimity I had not previously known. *My passion turned into dispassion, leading to the reduction of several likes and attachments, as well as dislikes and aversions.*

The image that clarifies this is a seesaw. Joy sits on one end; sorrow on the other. To climb to the high seat of joy is to guarantee an equal fall to the other side — it is one motion. The equanimity Gurudev was cultivating was not an absence of life; it was the stable fulcrum at the centre, where neither end had a claim. *"If you want to reduce sorrow, you must first reduce joy."* That is not deprivation. That is simply arithmetic about how the seesaw works.

**The Paradox of Full Belonging**

All of those who came to Gurudev shared a private conviction: that we were his closest. He seemed to love each of us more carefully than our own parents ever had. He spent more time with us than with his own children. The feeling was not imagined — it was real in its effect, and it bound us to the work.

But much later, understanding him better, I saw what it actually was:

> *All of Gurudev's followers believed he was closest to them. I did too. We felt he loved and cared for us far more than our parents ever did. And he spent more time with us than he did with his own children. Much later, when I got to know him better, I realised that he belonged to everyone, and yet, to no one. He was ready to relieve our pain, but not feel it.*
> — *Aatma Sutra*, Ch.IX, p.101

He expressed this himself with a precision I have not found elsewhere:

> *"Main sab ka hoon aur sab mere hain.
> Lekin main kisi ka nahi hu aur mera koi nahi."*
> *(I belong to all, and all belong to me. Yet I belong to no one, and no one belongs to me.)*
> — Gurudev, *The Guru of Gurus*, p.108

The first line and the second line look contradictory. They are not. Because he belonged to no one in particular, he could belong to everyone equally. The love was present — attentive, particular to each one of us — but there was no emotional ownership underneath it. *Vairagya* at its peak does not produce withdrawal. It produces availability without limit.

**Where It Begins**

The sweater was where it began for me — a garment worn not for warmth but for impression management. Each of those small performances is a hook, a place where the 'I' insists on being seen in a particular way. Detachment does not start in an ashram, in the relinquishing of family or property. It starts in noticing the next small thing you do or withhold not because it is true but because of the audience in your own head.

*"Grihasth mein bhi vairagya hai."* The path is open from here.
