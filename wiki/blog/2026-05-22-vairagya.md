I sat shivering in my sweater at the Mungaoli offsite camp, and it was entirely my own doing. The evening was cold, and almost everyone around me was wrapped in cardigans and shawls. I had arrived wearing a sweater too — but as Gurudev's jeep pulled up, I did a quick calculation: if I was the only one without a warm layer, he might think I was putting on a display of hardiness. Social vanity is a subtle thing. I kept the sweater on to avoid being perceived as showing off. Gurudev read me in a glance. Within minutes, I was chattering with a chill that went well beyond what the night air justified. He had turned my pretence into reality — body, mind, and atmosphere cooperating to make the lesson unmistakeable. Surrender includes surrendering the small social performances. His words from those camp years stayed with me long after the cold did: *"Grihasth mein bhi vairagya hai."* There is renunciation within the householder life too.

**What Attachment Actually Costs**

The sweater was only the surface of it. The deeper teaching is about understanding what it costs to be emotionally owned by the world. Every attachment you carry becomes a liability: if you love with possession, every threat to what you love becomes your private catastrophe. If your self-image is invested in an outcome, its failure is your failure. The mind swings between elation and grief — a pendulum that cannot settle, because the very act of clinging to the good guarantees that the pendulum must swing the other way. *Aatma Sutra* states it plainly:

> "If you aspire to higher levels of consciousness, then you need to detach not only from family, friends, and material objects but also from your own perceived identity."
> — Hingori, *Aatma Sutra*, Ch.IX, p.101

The surprise in that sentence is the last phrase. It is relatively easy to see the cost of attachment to objects. The attachment to identity — to the story of who you are, to the performance of self — is harder to spot precisely because it masquerades as sincerity.

**What It Looks Like at Its Peak**

To understand what this costs — and what becomes possible when you give it up — look at Gurudev. All of his followers believed he was closest to them. I did too. We felt he loved and cared for us far more than our own parents ever had, and he spent more time with us than he did with his own children. Much later, when I got to know him better, I realised that he belonged to everyone, and yet, to no one. *He was ready to relieve our pain, but not feel it.*

He stated his own position in words I have not found bettered:

> *"Main sab ka hoon aur sab mere hain.*
> *Lekin main kisi ka nahi hu aur mera koi nahi."*
> (I belong to all, and all belong to me.
> Yet I belong to no one, and no one belongs to me.)
> — Gurudev, *The Guru of Gurus*, p.108

The paradox is structural, not merely poetic. Because he was not possessed by any one of us, he could be available to all of us equally. The love was real in its effect — every disciple genuinely felt uniquely cared for — but there was no emotional ownership underneath it. Love as function, not as feeling. This is *vairagya* at full integration. It does not produce coldness; it produces unlimited availability. Only those who ask nothing of a relationship can give themselves to it completely.

**To Conquer Sorrow, First Conquer Happiness**

I have described Gurudev's state. Here is what he did to bring me toward the edge of it. In the first four years of my time as a potential disciple, Gurudev's opening act was to use his eraser on me. I lost complete faith in my own thinking, my attitude towards almost everything. All I had ever learnt seemed irrelevant. I didn't know what to think. He had begun the process of unlearning.

The first thing he taught me was this: *if you desire to conquer sorrow, you must first conquer happiness.* For four long years, I practised no laughter, no joy, no enjoyment at home, work, or parties, and no reaction to good news of any kind. It was almost like deliberately deadening the emotions — to arrive at boredom, and through boredom, at equanimity. As a corollary, passion turned into dispassion. Likes softened. Attachments thinned. Dislikes and aversions too. What emerged was not despondency. It was the slow lifting of the veil of maya, an induction into non-duality.

I do not pretend I completed the journey. I still have to work towards extinguishing the desire for the sense of touch and taste. I accept that — and am satisfied with the results.

**The Householder Who Renounces**

This is often misunderstood, and I should say so plainly, because the word *renunciation* can frighten people out of the inquiry. *Vairagya* does not require leaving your family, your work, or your world. It requires changing your relationship to all of it. Gurudev demonstrated this as precisely as anyone I have witnessed. He lived in 120-square-foot bachelorhood rooms and makeshift camp tents without distress. He inhabited every role — father, employer, healer, friend — with full engagement, without being colonised by any of them. He was away from his family for extended periods and returned without the guilt and self-pity that such absences tend to carry. He lived the life of a renunciate inside the householder path. His was not a renunciation of the world but a renunciation of the world's claim over him.

The practice does not begin with grand gestures. It begins with the sweater. With noticing, in some small social moment, the calculation you are making to manage how you appear — and asking what it would cost you, just this once, to let that calculation go.

*Vairagya* is not a destination. It is the direction you point yourself in — again, and again, and again.
