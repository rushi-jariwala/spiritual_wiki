One winter morning at one of Gurudev's biannual offsite seva camps, I made a small, dishonest decision. Almost every other disciple had bundled up in cardigans and woollens. I had arrived in a sweater. Rather than acknowledge the cold, I left the sweater on — not because I was warm, but because I did not want Gurudev to think I was showing off by pretending not to feel it. The pretence was its own vanity, reversed.

He read it instantly. By turning my social performance into reality, he made the cold *real* — I shivered and chattered for the rest of the evening. As I sat rattling in my sweater, the lesson arrived fully formed: surrender to the mahaguru included the surrender of false ego, social graces, and make-believe. His own words put it plainly, years later: *"Grihasth mein bhi vairagya hai"* — there is renunciation even within the householder's life. *(→ [[stories/mungaoli-sweater-incident|The Mungaoli Sweater]])*

**What detachment actually is**

The word *vairagya* tends to conjure an image — an ascetic walking away from the world, from family, from all comfort. That image is incomplete. *Aatma Sutra* puts it precisely:

> "If you aspire to higher levels of consciousness, then you need to detach not only from family, friends, and material objects but also from your own perceived identity."
> — Hingori, *Aatma Sutra*, Ch.IX, p.101

That last clause is the harder one. Most of us can imagine, at least theoretically, loosening our grip on things. But loosening the grip on *who we think we are* — on the personality, the reputation, the small social performances we put on each morning — is the deeper work. My sweater incident was trivial. The principle it illustrated was not.

*Vairagya* is not indifference and it is not withdrawal. It is a change in how you inhabit what you are already inside. The pleasure-pain seesaw continues to operate for everyone — but the question is where you are standing on it. As long as you are perched at one end, the other end will inevitably come up. Reduce joy enough, and sorrow has less to balance. The fulcrum — the still centre — is not a joyless state. It is bliss: the emotion-free ground that underlies both joy and sorrow without belonging to either.

**The paradox Gurudev lived**

To understand what this looks like in a human life, I had only to watch Gurudev. All of his followers believed, without exception, that he was closest to *them*. I believed this too. We felt he loved and cared for us far more than our own parents. Much later, I realised the source of that feeling: he belonged to everyone, and yet to no one. He was ready to relieve our pain, but not feel it.

He said it himself, in the language he reserved for the clearest truths:

> *"Main sab ka hoon aur sab mere hain.*
> *Lekin main kisi ka nahi hu aur mera koi nahi."*
>
> (I belong to all, and all belong to me.
> Yet I belong to no one, and no one belongs to me.)
> — Gurudev, *The Guru of Gurus*, p.108

The two lines are not a contradiction. They are the same state, seen from opposite angles. Because he was not owned by any particular person, he could be fully available to every person. Love as a function — not as a feeling that could run out, be withheld, or play favourites.

**What the practice asks of us**

When Gurudev began the serious work of training my mind, he gave me an instruction that sounded impossible: if I wished to conquer sorrow, I had first to conquer happiness. For four years, I practised no laughter, no joy, no reaction to good news of any kind. It was, as I described it then, almost like deadening the emotions to attain boredom. The boredom was very boring. But what it produced was equanimity — a state in which my passion turned, quietly, into dispassion.

> All I had ever learnt, until then, seemed irrelevant. I did not know what to think, walked around confused, not knowing how to get a grip on my life. He had begun to use his eraser and the process of unlearning had started.
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.9, p.89

Over the years, I lost interest in some of the things I was once passionate about. That is not loss — it is the beginning of *vairagya*. What I was losing was not the things themselves but my emotional ownership of them, the part of me that needed them to stay, to be admired, to be praised. Gurudev knew, from lifetimes of watching souls on this path, exactly what I was and what I would eventually become. His eraser was not arbitrary. He was clearing ground.

*(→ [[stories/hingori-mind-training-non-duality|Four Years of Destroying the Mind]])*

*Vairagya* does not ask you to become less. It asks you to notice how much of what you call "you" is a sweater you're wearing for someone else's benefit — and then, slowly, to take it off.
