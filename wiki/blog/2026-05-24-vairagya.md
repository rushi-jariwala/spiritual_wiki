I arrived at an offsite camp one winter wearing a sweater. Not because I was cold — I was not — but because every other disciple was bundled into their cardigans, and I did not want to look as though I were showing off by going without. A small social pretence, perfectly invisible, or so I imagined. Within minutes I was shivering violently. The cold I had been performing had become real. Gurudev had read what no one else could see, and he turned my make-believe into fact. He said nothing. Later, in a moment of its own, the teaching arrived: *Grihasth mein bhi vairagya hai.* There is renunciation within the householder's life too.

**The Surrender of Small Vanities**

That shivering was a lesson not about cold but about ego — the particular variety that wants to appear unaffected without actually being unaffected. This is where *vairagya* begins: not at the monastery gate, but at the social performance, the small claimed identity, the impression carefully managed. *Aatma Sutra* puts it directly: detachment is asked not only from family, friends, and material objects, but ultimately from one's own perceived identity. The outer layer — desires, pleasures, commercial attachments — is manageable with effort. The inner layer is harder: the body, the personality, the 'I' that assigns authorship to everything.

What makes *vairagya* so commonly misunderstood is that it looks, from the outside, like coldness. It is not. It is the shift from owning one's experience to being fully present to it without ownership. The difference is more significant than it sounds. The one who owns the feeling of love suffers when it is withdrawn. The one who is present to it — fully present, without claiming it — can give it endlessly, because nothing is taken when it moves on.

**The Man Who Belonged to Everyone**

To understand this at its fullest, consider what I observed in Gurudev across decades of discipleship. All of his followers — hundreds of them — believed he was closest to them. I believed the same. We felt he loved and cared for us far more than our parents ever did, and spent more time with us than with his own children. It was only much later that I understood: *he belonged to everyone, and yet, to no one. He was ready to relieve our pain, but not feel it.*

He had said it once himself:

*"Main sab ka hoon aur sab mere hain.*
*Lekin main kisi ka nahi hu aur mera koi nahi."*

I belong to all, and all belong to me. Yet I belong to no one, and no one belongs to me.

The paradox is not a contradiction — it is the same state described from opposite directions. Because he was emotionally attached to no one in particular, he could be fully available to everyone equally. *Practising detachment from the outcome of a deed helped make seva a habit. That meant there was no joy to be found in it and no pain to be felt doing it. It was a duty that needed to be carried out without emotion and with utmost humility.* He had role-played affection to perfection, and in doing so had given each disciple exactly what they needed — without the giving costing him anything, and without the receiving making anyone more or less than equal in his eyes.

**The Long Practice**

Understanding this was one thing. Living it was another. In the first four years of my discipleship, Gurudev's opening act was to systematically dismantle my mind. I lost faith in my style of thinking, my attitudes, almost everything I had learned until then. He had, as I later came to understand it, picked up his eraser.

What he taught me first was this: to conquer sorrow, I had to conquer happiness. Consider a seesaw. Raga — attraction — is at one end; dwesa — aversion — is at the other. Every swing toward one end guarantees an equal swing toward the other. Joy produces its balancing sorrow; pleasure its balancing pain. It is only at the still, emotion-free centre — the fulcrum — that neither dominates. That centre is what Gurudev was training me toward. For four long years I practised no laughter, no joy, no enjoyment at home, work, or parties. No reaction to good news of any kind. It was his deliberate induction into boredom — which is also, at its core, a state of equanimity of the mind.

The outcome was not despondency. *As a corollary, my passion turned into dispassion, leading to the reduction of several likes and attachments, as well as dislikes and aversions.* It was my story of slowly lifting the veil of maya, and being inducted into non-duality. I will admit I have not given it all up yet. But I accept that, and am satisfied with what has come of it.

**What It Makes Possible**

The strange thing about *vairagya* — the thing I could not have understood at the beginning — is what it frees you for. Attachment to outcome constricts. It makes seva conditional, love transactional, giving a form of investment. When the attachment is reduced, the service simply is. It asks nothing of the recipient. It expects nothing in return. It does not even require the feeling of giving to sustain itself.

This is what Gurudev carried in him when, as a young man, he waded through knee-deep floodwater every Thursday to light a chirag at the dargah, shielding the flame with his body against the wind. Not once did devotion appear as effort. The ritual was simply what Thursday was — because the person performing it had no stake in whether it was noticed, remembered, or returned.

*(→ [[stories/gurudev-chirag-flood|Gurudev Wades the Flood to Light the Chirag]])*

Detachment does not empty life. It clears the channel through which it can flow.
