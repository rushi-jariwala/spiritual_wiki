It was one of those biannual offsite camps where the accommodation was deliberately modest — decrepit district guest houses, makeshift tents on open ground, 120-square-foot rooms for a group of disciples and their teacher. I had come wrapped against the cold, but discreetly. Everyone around me had pulled on cardigans and shawls openly; I had hidden my sweater beneath my shirt, unwilling to be seen as someone who needed warming. The pretence was its own kind of vanity — performing endurance by concealing comfort.

Gurudev saw straight through it. Within minutes I was shivering in a way that had nothing to do with the weather: a full-body rattle, cold crawling down my spine, my hidden sweater entirely useless. He had made the pretence real. By turning my fabrication into actual experience, he stripped away not the sweater but what lay underneath — the small, restless ego that was calculating how I appeared to others even in the middle of a spiritual retreat.

*"Grihasth mein bhi vairagya hai,"* he said. There is renunciation within the householder life too.

**What Vairagya Is Not**

That sentence has lived inside me for decades. Most people, on hearing the word *vairagya*, picture a renunciate: someone who has left family, possessions, and the world entirely behind. Gurudev's teaching pointed in the opposite direction — renunciation within the householder life, detachment not as withdrawal from the world but as a different way of inhabiting it.

*Aatma Sutra* describes the full scope: *"If you aspire to higher levels of consciousness, then you need to detach not only from family, friends, and material objects but also from your own perceived identity."*

The second part is the harder one. Most of us can at least aspire to loosen our grip on objects. Letting go of the perceived identity — the personality we have spent a lifetime constructing and now defend at every provocation — that is the real frontier of vairagya.

**The Seesaw**

To understand why that frontier matters, consider the mechanism behind attachment itself. Every bond — to a person, to an outcome we feel we deserve, to the self-image we project in public — places us on a seesaw. One end is joy; the other is sorrow. *"It is only at the centre of a seesaw that emotions are at equilibrium. There is no joy at the centre, but then there is also no sadness. If you want to reduce sorrow, you must first reduce joy."*

This is the logic that Gurudev taught me through an extended process I did not fully understand until I had reached the other side of it. For four years at the beginning of my discipleship, he systematically dismantled my relationship with pleasure and pain — not by forbidding anything, but by training me to stop claiming ownership of my emotional reactions. His first instruction: *if I desired to conquer sorrow, I had to conquer happiness.* Four years of no laughter, no enjoyment, no reaction to good news of any kind. What arrived on the other side felt — at first — like a very boring kind of equanimity. But passion turned to dispassion. Strong attachments dissolved on their own, no longer re-energised by the 'I' that had been claiming them. He called it slowly lifting the veil of maya, being inducted into non-duality.

**Belonging to All, Belonging to None**

I saw the fullest expression of all this in Gurudev himself. All of his followers believed he was closest to them — I did too. We felt he loved and cared for us far more than our parents ever had. Much later, when I got to know him better, I realised that he belonged to everyone, and yet to no one. *He was ready to relieve our pain, but not feel it.*

He said it in his own words:

> *"Main sab ka hoon aur sab mere hain.*
> *Lekin main kisi ka nahi hu aur mera koi nahi."*
>
> *(I belong to all, and all belong to me.*
> *Yet I belong to no one, and no one belongs to me.)*

The paradox is the teaching. Unlimited belonging and complete non-attachment are not opposites — they are the same state described from two directions. Because he was not emotionally possessed by any one of us, he could be fully available to all. He had applied the same principle to the outcomes of his own seva: *there was no joy to be found in it and no pain to be felt doing it. It was a duty that needed to be carried out without emotion and with utmost humility.*

I saw this once at an offsite dinner. After everyone had eaten and only a small portion remained in the kadai, he walked uninvited into the kitchen, sat down on the spot, divided the last serving in two, and insisted that the young woman who had cooked it eat with him. He finished with a smile and turned to his daughter Renu ji, who had been watching from the doorway: *"Putt, jennu khellaan che mazaa agaya na, khaane da mazaa nahin rehnda."* — One who learns the pleasure of feeding others, seldom cares about feeding oneself.

*(→ [[stories/gurudev-indu-subzi-mussoorie|Gurudev, Indu ji, and the Last Portion of Subzi]])*

**A Direction, Not a Destination**

What I have described so far is the destination — vairagya in its fully realised form. But the practice itself is a direction, not a switch. I have walked in it for decades and will tell you candidly: I have not given it all up yet. The aspiration is not sudden severance but a progressive loosening — something released here, something held less tightly there — until the seesaw begins to still at its centre.

Begin with the sweater calculation. Notice where you are performing something for an audience that exists mainly in your own mind. That is where the practice starts — not in forests or monasteries, but in the precise moment when you catch the ego managing its appearance among people who probably did not notice anyway.

Take your sweater off.
