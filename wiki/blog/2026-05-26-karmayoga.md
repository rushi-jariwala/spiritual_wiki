In 1988, a senior scientist from IARI — a man who had spent decades advising India's agricultural policy — went down on his knees in an arid field near Gurgaon. Not to inspect. Not to advise. To till.

His disciple Giri ji had purchased a plot of land in Mohammedpur village that most farmers had already abandoned. The soil was parched, the yield negligible, the terrain quietly written off. Gurudev showed up with his hands. He tilled the soil, ploughed the field, sowed seeds, and planted trees. Within months, the land turned green. The neighbouring farmers — those who had quietly given up — saw what had become of Giri ji's plot and returned to their own. A village that had almost died came back to life.

*(→ [[stories/gurudev-mohammedpur-greening|Gurudev Greens the Land at Mohammedpur]])*

There were blisters on his hands. He did not stop.

**The Arithmetic of Service**

This was not charity dispensed from a comfortable distance. Gurudev had a principle he stated with characteristic economy: *"Luxury is an expense in spiritualism."* He chose blistered hands over ease not out of mortification, but because he had calculated the actual cost. Every comfort draws on a budget of spiritual energy; whatever you spend there is unavailable for service. He was simply managing the account.

This is what I understand by karmayoga: the discipline of performing good karma not as an occasional act of virtue, but as a structure of living — done with the awareness that the accounting runs across lifetimes, not just this one. The deed you perform today seeds your future, and the future of the life that follows, and the one after that.

Emperor Ashoka understood this, though he arrived at it along a very different road. After the Kalinga war — the slaughter that never left him — he turned the entire machinery of an empire toward service. The words he left behind are ones I have carried since school:

> *"Work I must for the common weal and the way to achieve is by effort and dispatch; to this end I toil to discharge my debt to human beings and to make some happy; in this world, and the next."*
> — Emperor Ashoka, *Karma Sutra*, Ch.1, p.15

*"In this world, and the next."* That last clause is what separates the karmayogi from the ordinary philanthropist. Ashoka was not merely being generous; he was tending the ledger of his jivaatma across time.

**The Sleepless Night**

To understand what karmayoga actually costs — and what makes it worth the cost — I think of a night that Gurudev did not speak about easily.

In the early 1970s, during a market visit with his family, his daughter Renu asked for two glasses of milk from a sweetmeat shop. She drank them with such obvious delight that Gurudev turned to Mataji and asked whether milk was regularly available at home. The reply stopped him cold: there was never quite enough money for the children's dietary essentials. He had been pouring himself into service while his own family went without milk.

He hardly slept that night. The question kept returning — had he chosen rightly? Was the service worth what it had cost the people closest to him?

By morning, he had his answer. *Service before self* became, from that day, the settled backbone of everything he built. Not because the family had ceased to matter. Because he understood, at a level that went past argument, that the kind of debt created by genuine service is the only debt worth incurring.

*(→ [[stories/renu-milk-market-visit|Renu's Milk — The Night of Doubt]])*

Spirituality was his chosen line, and he went the whole nine yards. Seva became the cashmere with which he knit his goals — not ornament, but the fibre that gave the entire enterprise its warmth and its capacity to last.

**What Outlasts the Body**

I would not have believed any of this on theory alone. What finally convinced me — almost forty years ago now — was a visit to a Parsi gentleman in Andheri, Mumbai, before Gurudev had cured my arthritis.

His name was Mr. Marchant: a retired railway employee who used a planchette apparatus to receive instructions from his late guru before prescribing homeopathic treatment. He never charged a penny for the medicine or the consultation. His late guru had forbidden it.

The day I came to give him the good news — that Gurudev had cured me — he set up the planchette as usual. The pendulum swung with far more vigour than I had ever seen it, nearly ninety degrees, almost aggressive. Mr. Marchant broke into what appeared to be a conversation with his apparatus, forgetting entirely that I was in the room. Eventually he looked up, helpless: *"He is saying you are alright. Is it true?"*

I decided to press further. How had I been cured? The pendulum moved again. *"Jungle man, jungle medicine,"* he said. And the extent? *"95 per cent."*

Mr. Marchant's guru had been dead for years. He was still healing people through his disciple. Still refusing fees. Still accumulating what I can only describe as post-death karmas on the other side of the veil.

> *"His guru was earning post-death karmas by healing people and not allowing Mr. Marchant to charge for it. It strengthened my belief in karmayoga, almost 40 years back."*
> — Hingori, *Karma Sutra*, Ch.5, p.87

*(→ [[stories/marchant-planchette-healing|Mr Marchant and the Planchette — Post-Death Karma in Action]])*

Karmayoga does not ask you to wait for an empire or a farm. It begins the moment you act with awareness — knowing that each deed plants a seed, that seeds outlast seasons, and that the harvest may not arrive in this life at all.

Sow accordingly.
