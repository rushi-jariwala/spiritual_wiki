At one of Gurudev's offsite camps, I walked in wearing a sweater. The others were bundled in cardigans; I had instinctively pulled one on too, but something in me did not want him to think I was performing hardiness — showing off a cold-immunity I didn't actually possess. So I kept the sweater on: a small social calculation, invisible to everyone, or so I thought.

Gurudev said nothing. Within the hour, I was shivering so violently I could barely speak — a chill that arrived by intention, settling into the bones well beyond what the weather could account for. When it was over and I had understood what had happened, five words came. Not a lecture, not a formal teaching: *"Grihasth mein bhi vairagya hai."* There is renunciation within the householder life too.

It took me years to fully unpack this. At that moment, all I grasped was that surrender to the mahaguru required surrendering the social graces, the small performances, the layers of false ego I hadn't even known I was wearing.

**The Knot Beneath the Bond**

*Vairagya* is commonly translated as detachment — and that word carries the wrong flavour. It sounds like withdrawal, the cold shoulder of the spiritual aspirant turning away from the warmth of relationship. That is not what it is. *Aatma Sutra* puts it squarely: *"If you aspire to higher levels of consciousness, then you need to detach not only from family, friends, and material objects but also from your own perceived identity."* (Ch.IX, p.101) The target is not the relationship but the grip beneath it — the perceived identity that clutches, that says *this is mine*, that makes another's pain automatically your own.

Attachment locks you into the pleasure-pain cycle. Your spouse's anxieties land in your chest; your child's disappointments become yours to carry. This is not love — it is entanglement dressed as love. *Vairagya* does not cut the relationship; it loosens the knot underneath it, so you can be fully present without being swept away.

**The Love That Could Not Choose**

Renu ji — Gurudev's eldest daughter — confronted him one day about his absence. There were stretches of ten to fifteen days, sometimes an entire month, when barely a word passed between them. Disciples came and went in waves; he was always available to them. She gathered her courage and told him plainly: he had no time for his own children. That Mataji was everything to them. That they could not survive without her.

He held her gaze for two minutes. Then: *"I do not know how to convey affection like your mother. One day you will realise, and then you will understand what true love is."* (*Witnessing Greatness*, Ch.6, p.80)

She did not understand while he was alive. The understanding arrived only in the aftermath of his passing.

*(→ [[stories/renu-gurudev-confrontation-on-love|Renu Ji Confronts Gurudev About Love]])*

What looks like absence from one angle is something else entirely from another. The narrator offers this gloss: love is more than an emotion. It is like radiation, permeating everything, unrestricted by boundaries — enveloping everyone like a fragrance, not a select few. Radiation does not choose its direction; it propagates in all directions and is not depleted by spreading. A fragrance does not decide which room it enters. Gurudev's love was structurally incapable of exclusion, which meant it was also structurally incapable of preference. This is what he was pointing to when he said, in the most precise formulation in the corpus:

*"Main sab ka hoon aur sab mere hain.*
*Lekin main kisi ka nahi hu aur mera koi nahi."*

I belong to all, and all belong to me. Yet I belong to no one, and no one belongs to me.

(*The Guru of Gurus*, p.108)

The two lines are not in tension — they describe the same condition from opposite directions. Because he belonged to no one in particular, he could belong to everyone equally. His family bore the cost of this; his disciples received its benefit. This is *vairagya* at full integration — not a void where feeling should be, but a love so complete it could not be parcelled.

**The Interior Work**

Understanding this is one thing; inhabiting it is another. What Gurudev lived effortlessly required of me four deliberate years of construction. His opening act as a teacher was to use his eraser — to dismantle the scaffolding of my thinking, my attitudes, my ways of reading everything. I walked around confused, unable to get a grip on my life. Everything I had built until then seemed suddenly irrelevant.

The first thing he taught me was this: to conquer sorrow, I had first to conquer happiness. For four long years, I practised no laughter, no joy, no enjoyment — at home, at work, at parties — no reaction to good news of any kind. It was almost like deadening the emotions to arrive at boredom. That boredom was very boring, and yet it was a state of equanimity. Through it, my passion turned slowly into dispassion; several attachments, and equally several aversions, quietly reduced. I was slowly lifting the veil of maya and being inducted into something approaching non-duality. (*Guru Sutra*, Ch.9, pp.89–91)

*(→ [[stories/hingori-mind-training-non-duality|Four Years of Destroying the Mind]])*

Do not mistake this for a story of despondency. It is a story of discovering that the self I had been defending was not the self worth protecting.

**"We Are Fakirs"**

Back at the family home: Renu ji had petitioned Gurudev for more space — a private drawing and dining room for the family. After some gentle resistance, he agreed. A new floor was built. When it was complete, he walked through it and remarked with quiet satisfaction: *"It is perfect, and about 20 to 25 people can sleep here comfortably."* (*Witnessing Greatness*, Ch.6, pp.84–85)

The room she had imagined as family retreat became, in one sentence, communal sleeping space for disciples. He turned to her and offered three words by way of explanation: *"We are fakirs."* He had not refused the room — he had accepted it while redefining its purpose entirely. Luxury, personal comfort, private sanctuary: these were categories that simply did not occupy him. He was a grihasth and, in every meaningful sense, a renunciate — both at once, without contradiction.

*(→ [[stories/gurudev-drawing-room-fakirs|The Drawing Room for Twenty-Five]])*

The sweater at the camp, I eventually understood, was not about the cold. It was about every small layer of self-image we pile on unconsciously — the social performances, the false modesty, the ego pretences that masquerade as consideration for others. *Vairagya* does not ask you to leave the world. It asks you to notice, one layer at a time, what you are wearing underneath the sweater.
