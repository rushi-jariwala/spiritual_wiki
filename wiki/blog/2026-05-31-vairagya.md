Gurudev's eldest daughter had to muster the courage, one day, to say what the rest of the family had felt for years. Stretches of ten to fifteen days — sometimes an entire month — would pass with barely a word between father and child. The crowds outside the house never thinned; disciples always came first.

*"I told him he did not have time for us,"* Renu ji recalled. *"That Mummy was everything to us and we could not survive without her."* He locked his gaze on her for two minutes. She braced for his displeasure. Instead, his reply was quiet: *"I do not know how to convey affection like your mother. One day you will realise, and then you will understand what true love is."*

It took his death for the meaning to arrive.

**The Paradox**

The word for what Gurudev lived is *vairagya* — detachment. In popular usage the word sounds like withdrawal: someone closing themselves off, pulling back. His life proves the opposite. Every follower felt, privately, that he loved them more than all the others. He spent more time with us than with his own children, and each of us believed that wholeheartedly. The truth, which I understood only much later, was that he belonged to everyone because he belonged to no one.

He was ready to relieve our pain, but not to feel it.

He expressed this himself in one of the few direct statements he ever made about his own nature:

> *"Main sab ka hoon aur sab mere hain.*
> *Lekin main kisi ka nahi hu aur mera koi nahi."*
> (I belong to all, and all belong to me.
> Yet I belong to no one, and no one belongs to me.)
> — *The Guru of Gurus*, p.108

Both lines are true simultaneously. That is the structural paradox of *vairagya* at its complete expression: infinite availability and zero attachment are not in tension — they are the same condition, named from two sides.

**Grihasth Mein Bhi Vairagya Hai**

To understand this more concretely, consider one morning at Gurudev's offsite seva camps in Mungaoli. I wore a sweater — not because I was cold, but because nearly everyone else had their cardigans on and I did not want to appear to be showing off. He read the pretence instantly and made the cold real. I shivered through my sweater for as long as the lesson required. Surrender to the mahaguru, I eventually understood, includes the sacrifice of false ego, social graces, and make-believe.

His closing observation on the subject was four words long: *"Grihasth mein bhi vairagya hai."* There is renunciation within the householder life too.

*(→ [[stories/mungaoli-sweater-incident|The Mungaoli Sweater]])*

His children received the same teaching in their own home. Renu ji eventually persuaded Gurudev to build an additional floor — a proper drawing and dining room for the family. When the room was complete, he walked through it, looked around, and announced: *"Perfect. About twenty to twenty-five people can sleep here comfortably."*

She had imagined a private family space. He saw communal sleeping quarters.

*"We are fakirs,"* he reminded her gently.

*(→ [[stories/gurudev-drawing-room-fakirs|The Drawing Room for Twenty-Five]])*

**The Love That Asks Nothing**

*Vairagya* is not the absence of care. It is care with no emotional ownership underneath it. What we ordinarily call love carries an agenda — a need to be needed, a stake in the outcome, a private account running in the background. Gurudev's love had none of this. He role-played affection to perfection, making each disciple believe they were uniquely cherished — while the love beneath it was entirely even and impersonal.

*Aatma Sutra* is precise on this point: to aspire to higher levels of consciousness, one must detach not only from family, friends, and material objects, but also from one's own perceived identity. The outer detachment — releasing possessions, pleasures, and social performances — is the first step. The harder step is the inner one: releasing the 'I' that claims ownership over the acts of love themselves.

When that second step is taken, what remains is what Renu ji finally understood after her father was gone:

> *"Love is more than an emotion. It is like radiation permeating everything, unrestricted by boundaries. It should envelop everyone like a fragrance, not just a select few."*
> — *Witnessing Greatness*, Ch.6, p.80

A fragrance does not choose whom to reach. His absence as a father was the structural cost of a love that belonged to no one — and therefore to everyone.

The practice of *vairagya* does not begin with grand renunciation. It begins with the sweater: the small performance you are maintaining right now because you are afraid of what remains if you stop.
