I once wore a sweater I did not need. It was one of Gurudev's biannual camps — cold enough that almost everyone else had wrapped themselves in cardigans and shawls. I had come prepared, but when Gurudev arrived, I quietly removed it. I did not want him to think I was making a point of my hardiness. I wanted, in that small foolish way, to manage the impression I made on him.

He saw the pretence at once. By the time I understood what was happening, I was shivering — teeth chattering, chills down my spine — while wearing the very sweater I had meant to leave off. He had turned my performance into the reality. The cold I had been pretending not to feel, I now felt entirely.

When the embarrassment finally cooled to comprehension, the instruction landed: surrender to the mahaguru includes the sacrifice of false ego, social graces, and make-believe. *"Grihasth mein bhi vairagya hai,"* he said that day — there is renunciation within the householder life too.

*(→ [[stories/mungaoli-sweater-incident|The Mungaoli Sweater]])*

That is vairagya. Not the dramatic exit from the world. Not the ochre robe or the shaved head. Detachment is the quietly radical act of removing the emotional stake from everything you still fully inhabit.

**The Fakir in the Drawing Room**

To understand what this looks like across a life, you need only look at Gurudev's home.

As his family grew and the flow of visitors multiplied, Renu ji — his eldest daughter — petitioned him for a drawing room: private family space, somewhere to breathe without disciples arriving at all hours. Gurudev resisted at first. "What difference does it make? You can make do with the space you have." But eventually he relented, and one additional floor was built.

The family waited for their drawing room.

Gurudev walked in, looked around, and said: *"It is perfect, and about 20 to 25 people can sleep here comfortably."* And then, to Renu ji's stunned silence: *"We are fakirs."*

*(→ [[stories/gurudev-drawing-room-fakirs|The Drawing Room for Twenty-Five]])*

He had not refused the addition. He accepted it without hesitation — and then quietly redefined whose it was. The room Renu ji had imagined as a private family retreat became a communal sleeping hall. This is the vairagya of the grihasth: not abandoning the home, but never letting the home become *yours* in the possessive sense. He built; he did not own.

**The Love That Belongs to No One**

The hardest version of this teaching arrives in another of Renu ji's memories — this one costing far more than a drawing room.

Gurudev spent long stretches away from the family: two weeks at a time, sometimes an entire month, barely exchanging a word with his children even when he was under the same roof. As his eldest, Renu ji felt his absence most acutely. One day she found the courage to say it to his face — that he had no time for them, that they could not survive without their mother.

He held his gaze on her for two minutes. Then: *"I do not know how to convey affection like your mother. One day you will realise, and then you will understand what true love is."*

She did not understand then. She understood only after he died.

*(→ [[stories/renu-gurudev-confrontation-on-love|Renu Ji Confronts Gurudev About Love]])*

What he meant was this: *"Love is more than an emotion. It is like radiation permeating everything, unrestricted by boundaries. It should envelop everyone like a fragrance, not just a select few."* The love that concentrates on *this* family, *these* children, *my* people is warm inside its walls and cold outside them. The love that is detached — stripped of emotional ownership — becomes what the word actually promises: an undifferentiated presence that no one has to earn.

**The Paradox**

All of this — the sweater, the drawing room, Renu ji's unanswered question — points toward a paradox that Gurudev himself put into words directly:

> *Main sab ka hoon aur sab mere hain.
> Lekin main kisi ka nahi hu aur mera koi nahi.*
>
> (I belong to all, and all belong to me.
> Yet I belong to no one, and no one belongs to me.)
> — Gurudev, *The Guru of Gurus*, p.108

These two lines are not a contradiction. They describe the same state from opposite ends. Because he belonged to no one in particular, he could belong to everyone equally. All of his followers believed he was closest to them. I did too. We felt he loved and cared for us far more than our parents ever did. Much later, I understood the mechanism: he belonged to everyone, and yet to no one. He was ready to relieve our pain, but not feel it. He role-played affection to perfection, making each disciple believe they were uniquely loved. The truth was he loved everyone equally — but without an iota of emotion. Because there was no emotional ownership underneath it, the love could be given to all without preference and without depletion.

Every disciple who ever sat before him was right.

**What This Asks of Us**

Understanding the paradox points toward an equal and opposite misreading: detachment is not indifference. The cold parent who withholds affection while claiming to be above emotion is practising denial, not vairagya. Gurudev's example insists on the opposite: the detached person is *more* available, not less. He could be with everyone precisely because he was not consumed by anyone.

Nor is vairagya a retreat from the world. It is a direction, not a destination — a progressive loosening of the emotional grip on persons, objects, and finally on the very self-image that keeps claiming you. You do not renounce family and work. You inhabit them as roles, fully and without remainder, while finding your footing at the still centre where neither joy nor sorrow can drag you to either end of the seesaw.

The detachment Gurudev taught did not begin with grand gestures. It began with a sweater. With a drawing room. With an answer a daughter had to wait a lifetime to understand.

We are fakirs.
