There was a young woman in eighth-century Basra who had every reason to stop. Sold into slavery as a girl, forced by a desperate master into prostitution when famine made him desperate — she had been handed circumstances that would justify almost any form of giving up. Yet Rabia of Basra prayed without interruption, and prayed until she entered the silence I have come to call *dhyaan*: the still, voiceless country where the mind's argument with existence finally falls quiet. One evening her master noticed an ethereal lantern of light hovering above her head, radiating steadily in the dark. He freed her the next morning. She went on to become one of the most revered Sufi saints in history.

*(→ [[stories/rabia-of-basra-sufi-saint|Rabia of Basra — From Slave to Saint]])*

The question is not abstract. Could she have reached that state while blaming herself for being what circumstance made her? While filling the hours she gave to prayer with self-recrimination — cataloguing her own unworthiness, rehearsing every injustice, measuring herself against a life that was never offered to her? The answer is obvious. Self-blame would have consumed every drop of energy that the practice required. She had none to spare for it.

**The Gauntlet of Other People's Opinions**

Rabia's refusal of self-blame is striking, but it should not be unusual. What is unusual is how early, and how persistently, we are trained in the opposite direction. From the moment we arrive in a body, the world begins evaluating us — and the verdicts arrive faster than our ability to question them. Too young, then somehow too old. Not confident enough at first, then too arrogant once some confidence appears. Wrong family, wrong looks, wrong outcomes. The young are naive; the middle-aged are preoccupied; the older are no longer relevant. At no stage is a person granted what I would call a clean slate.

My grandfather, a man of fewer words than I have ever managed, had a phrase I have returned to for decades: *seerat ko dekho, surat ko nahi* — look at the inner qualities, not the outer beauty. He meant it as an instruction about how to see others. I have come to understand it as equally — more urgently — an instruction about how to see oneself. Most of us hear it and nod. Very few of us absorb it.

Guru Nanak put the same observation differently: *Nanak dukhiya sab sansaar* — the world is always in a state of unhappiness. Not a complaint. A description of what happens when we accept every external verdict as the final word on who we are.

**The Artist Does Not Accept the Insult**

Recognising the gauntlet is useful. The more insidious damage, though, is what we do internally once we absorb those evaluations — the self-blame we carry long after the original incident has passed, the guilt we replay in the unconscious until it programs itself as a *samskar*.

I recall a moment — one of those moments where something lands before you can filter it — when I heard a voice speak directly to me: *"Who are you to blame me? You are my painting. I am the artist. By blaming yourself, you are blaming me."*

Think of pure water poured into a cup with coffee, milk, and sugar. The water may feel, in some anthropomorphic sense, guilty of losing its purity — it has been adulterated. But the water has not ceased to be water. Coffee, milk, and sugar are additions; they can be removed. The process of *shuddhi* — purification — restores what was always there. Our circumstances, our mistakes, our guilt: these are the coffee and milk. The consciousness beneath is still water. To blame the water for what was poured into it is to misunderstand the chemistry entirely.

And the artist, let us be clear, does not take kindly to the painting blaming itself for the colours it was given.

**Two Guilts**

There is a subtler dimension to all this — one that cost me a particular moment of embarrassment before I understood it. My cousin Neena had come to me seeking help with a difficult domestic situation. She had not played fair in the relationship in question, and I had no moral standing to intervene. When, eventually, I could not end the conversation honestly, I told her — an outright lie — that Gurudev had asked me to help her. I felt the guilt the moment the words left my mouth. She replied, quietly, that yes, she knew. Gurudev had already confirmed it to her at the *sthan* in Gurgaon; she was grateful for my agreement. The guilt dissolved in an instant.

*(→ [[stories/hingori-neena-two-guilts|Hingori and Neena — Two Guilts Become None]])*

I had lied. The lie was also true. The two guilts cancelled each other, and what remained was a recognition I now carry as a working principle: *truth can neither be limited to, nor interpreted by the five senses. Being pronounced guilty by reference to the senses alone lacks perfection.* We are more often right — and more often aligned with something larger than our visible choices — than our guilt tells us.

Guilt at the conscious level will program a self-punishment into the *samskars* if you do not release it. It compounds the original error. It is the second wound, self-inflicted.

**The Line, Not the Point**

The dissolution of guilt at the level of single acts is one step. The deeper practice is learning not to judge yourself at any single cross-section at all.

Years ago, a father-in-law came to me with his son-in-law Rajeev's case — a man who had lost the family's wealth and responded by becoming an alcoholic and drug addict, living for years in a state of intoxication that had pulled his wife in with him. The father-in-law wanted to know what could be done. I told him: your son-in-law is a saint. Trust me — you will be proud of him, and he will do a great deal of good in this world. He looked at me the way people look at someone who has said something they will later regret. I added, you may laugh today — but he who laughs last, laughs the wisest.

Years later he came back to thank me. Rajeev had turned his life around completely — given up the intoxicants, thrown himself into social work and spiritual healing, and was by then respected by hundreds of people. Paliji was amused. "You were so right," he said. "But at that time, how could I have believed it?"

*(→ [[stories/rajeev-alcoholic-to-saint|Rajeev — From Alcoholic to Saint]])*

The truth, which I could see then and Paliji could not, is simple: Rajeev had always been that saint. The drunk years were one phase on a long line, not a final definition. The child at school, the drunk at his desk, the healer at the *sthan* — which one is Rajeev? All of them. None of them. The phases are gates; the jivaatma passes through each one, and none of them is the jivaatma.

A life is not a point. It is a line. And you are looking at yourself at a single cross-section and mistaking the snapshot for the portrait.

Self-acceptance is not approving of every version of yourself you have been or done. It is the recognition that you cannot be measured by any one of them.
