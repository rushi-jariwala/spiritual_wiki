I did not know what I was asking for. Puranji, a senior guru-bhai who had been in Gurudev's orbit for years, leaned over in the queue and asked what I intended to say when I reached the front. My rheumatoid arthritis had relapsed — the same affliction that had first brought me to Gurudev — and the honest answer was: a cure. But Puranji had other ideas. "Ask for guru kripa," he said. Two words I had never fully understood. They sounded poetic, relevant, exactly the sort of thing one ought to say to one's guru. I decided to say them.

The trouble was, Gurudev took them seriously.

He stopped the queue. He took me to a separate room. He pressed me, again and again, to name what I *truly* wanted — a boon, a favour, something concrete I could understand. I held to the phrase like a parrot. Not out of wisdom; I simply could not think of anything else. It turned into a battle of wits — the only one I ever won with Gurudev, and I won it not through cleverness but through vacancy.

Eventually, he grinned, pretended to give up, and patted me on the back. *"You will get a lot more than you can imagine."*

I walked away unimpressed. I had asked for a concept I could not grasp and received an assurance I did not believe. The arthritis was still there.

**What It Is Not**

The first lesson of guru kripa is what it is not. It is not social grace — the guru's warmth, his occasional generosity of tone, the way he might speak kindly to you in front of others. It is not a boon. It does not mean the teacher has favourites, or that certain disciples receive advantages others are denied. These are all confusions about the nature of grace.

The truth is more demanding. Guru kripa operates not at the level of personality but across all three of a disciple's bodies — physical, spirit, and causal — and all three of the guru's corresponding minds must have accepted you for it to flow. Being well-behaved, courteous, graceful, and generous may win favours. Attaining guru kripa is a deeper concept.

> "Sometimes, a reprimanding sentence from the guru can mean more than a profusion of kind words. Thus, guru kripa may not always be sweet and sugarcoated but can be astringent, like a bitter pill that cures."
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.12, p.115

Grace that only arrives as sweetness is a form of sentimentality. The guru's grace that actually shifts a disciple's trajectory can be indistinguishable from being corrected, being left to struggle, being given nothing at all for years. It is a bitter pill precisely because it cures.

**The Umbrella You Did Not Earn**

To understand what guru kripa does once it arrives, consider the umbrella metaphor from the *Karma Sutra*: the guru's blessings may not avert the rain of bad luck, but they provide an umbrella — neutralising the negativity of the planetary effects, extending the capacity to absorb what would otherwise have broken you. The rain still falls. The storms of prarabdh karma — illness, loss, difficulty — are not cancelled. What changes is what you can carry through them.

I have lived this. The decades since that room in Gurudev's house have not been free of difficulty. But within them, I found I could be of service not only to human beings, animals, and plants, but to their spirits and almost all forms of existence. Two words spoken without understanding produced everything I could not have imagined to ask for.

**What Nine Lifetimes Looks Like**

The most arresting teaching on guru kripa is its continuity. It does not belong to the body you are currently wearing.

> "A guru's grace on a person is not merely physical in nature but one that might be in continuity from previous lives and may continue to exist till the guru can guide the disciple towards the attainment of mukti."
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.12, p.117

Santlal ji had spent years trying to find his guru. He heard of a wise sadhu in Gangowal, in Punjab, and queued for hours every day for five days. On the fifth day, the sadhu confessed that he could not take him on — Santlal ji's spiritual allegiance lay elsewhere, with someone who was, at that very moment, in the same town. Years later, a business journey brought Santlal ji to Gurgaon. A midnight meeting led him to a man sitting cross-legged in a simple lungi, offering no outward signs of authority. Santlal ji was sceptical. He asked this man whether he had been to Gangowal in 1960. He had. The man described the sadhu perfectly. Then he told Santlal ji what no one had said before: that their bond extended across nine previous incarnations, and that his mother's blessings had brought their meeting forward by two full years.

Santlal ji had not found Gurudev. He had been found. The kripa was already running across nine lifetimes, waiting for the precise alignment of circumstance that would make the meeting possible. What arrives as a midnight coincidence in Gurgaon has roots that go back further than memory can reach.

**The Everyday Version**

Guru kripa does not always announce itself in dramatic encounters. A disciple named Karmakarji, from the Kolkata sthan, worked at a pharmaceutical company where cocktail parties were a professional obligation. He observed a personal restriction against alcohol on Thursdays. His company scheduled a party on a Thursday. He went, broke the restriction, and returned to his guru Dutta Babu to seek forgiveness. The guru told him not to be discouraged — but to use the power of intent to ensure no such event would fall on a Thursday again. Karmakarji willed it. For as long as he worked at that company, it never hosted a cocktail party on a Thursday. A coincidence, perhaps. But guru kripa, as Hingori observes, was a distinct possibility.

The closing verse of the chapter puts the purpose plainly:

> He fills the disciple with wisdom  
>      and sets his soul aglow  
>  so that one day the disciple can  
> re-ignite the flame in many more.  
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.12, p.126

Guru kripa is not for the disciple's comfort alone. It is for the eventual capacity to light someone else's flame. The grace moves through you, not merely toward you.

Ask for it. Even if — especially if — you do not yet understand what you are asking for.
