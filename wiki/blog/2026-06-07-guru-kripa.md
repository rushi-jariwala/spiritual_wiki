I had come to Gurudev the second time in need of a cure. Rheumatoid arthritis is not the most patient of companions, and by then it had made itself quite at home in my joints. Waiting in the queue, a senior disciple named Puranji asked me what I intended to ask for. I told him: the arthritis. He shook his head gently and suggested two words instead — *guru kripa*. I had no real idea what they meant, not truly. But they sounded right. When I stood before Gurudev at last, those two words were what came out.

What followed was unexpected. Gurudev stopped the queue, led me to a separate room, and insisted I ask for what I truly wanted. I held to those two words — not out of wisdom, but because I genuinely could not think of anything else. That was the only battle of wits I ever won with Gurudev, and I won it purely because I had nothing smarter to say. Eventually, he pretended to give up, patted me on the back, and said: *"You will get a lot more than you can imagine."*

I left unimpressed. I had arrived with a physical ailment and departed with a verbal concept. Was that a good deal?

It turned out to be the best deal of my life.

**What Guru Kripa Is — and Is Not**

Guru kripa does not mean social favour. It is not the guru being kind at dinner, including you in a conversation, or smiling at you across a room. These are pleasant; they are not kripa. *Guru Sutra* is direct on this:

> "Sometimes, a reprimanding sentence from the guru can mean more than a profusion of kind words. Thus, guru kripa may not always be sweet and sugarcoated but can be astringent, like a bitter pill that cures."
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.12, p.115

Guru kripa operates at the level of the guru's three minds — the physical, the mental, and the spiritual mind of his three bodies. For grace to flow to a disciple, all three of those minds must have accepted him. Being well-mannered, generous, or devoted may win you certain favours; none of that, by itself, is kripa. And this acceptance is not limited to a single lifetime:

> "A guru's grace on a person is not merely physical in nature but one that might be in continuity from previous lives and may continue to exist till the guru can guide the disciple towards the attainment of mukti."
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.12, p.117

The relationship, at its root, is not between two personalities. It is an arrangement between two jivaatmas — one further along the journey, one being quietly drawn forward.

**The Saint in the Drunk**

To understand what guru kripa actually looks like in practice, consider a man named Rajeev. He was brought to my sthan when he was, to put it plainly, a dissolute alcoholic. His wealth had collapsed, his wife had joined him in his intoxications, and his father-in-law arrived to ask whether anything could be done. I told the father-in-law something that left him momentarily speechless: "Your son-in-law is a saint. You will be proud of him. He will one day be respected and worshipped by hundreds and thousands of people."

His expression was priceless. I added, with a snide remark I could not resist: "You may laugh today, Paliji. But he who laughs last, laughs the wisest."

Years passed. Rajeev gave up alcohol, grew into a steadiness of character, and immersed himself in healing and social service. Paliji returned — his face entirely different — to thank me. I told him what I genuinely believed: *"I had done nothing to bring about the metamorphosis. It was his destiny and I, the trigger."*

The siddh guru does not see the cross-section. He sees the line. A seed looks nothing like the tree it will become — but the tree is already fully encoded within it. Guru kripa is often simply the act of seeing that clearly, and then watching a person grow toward what they already are.

**Thirty Years Outside the Door**

To understand how grace works across time, consider Dr. Vachhani. When he first came to me, I refused to meet him. He waited outside my office every day — catching me at entry and exit — with no invitation, no appointment, and no guarantee I would ever relent. Something about the quality of his persistence eventually became impossible to ignore. I began to oblige.

It has been a three-decade long journey with him. The conversations gradually shifted from spiritual theory to practice. Today, when allopathy reaches its limits with a patient, Dr. Vachhani sends them to the sthan — or sometimes, he simply wills them to improve.

*(→ [[stories/dr-vachhani-persistence|Dr. Vachhani's Persistence]])*

*"The graduation is not a flick-of-a-wand transformation, but a slow metamorphosis like that of a caterpillar into a butterfly, which enables it to emerge into its own splendour."* What Vachhani carries now — a physician who heals by intent — did not arrive in a day. It arrived across three decades of showing up.

**The Words That Grow Backward**

Looking back at that second meeting with Gurudev — the arthritis, the queue, Puranji's two words, the strange battle of wits in a side room — I can say with certainty that I could not have made a better choice. Not because I was wise. Because grace was already at work, and those two words were its opening move.

> He fills the disciple with wisdom
> and sets his soul aglow
> so that one day the disciple can
> re-ignite the flame in many more.
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.12, p.126

The two words you barely understand, the years spent outside the door, the prophecy that sounds absurd in the moment — these are not signs that grace is absent. They are often the first shapes it takes.
