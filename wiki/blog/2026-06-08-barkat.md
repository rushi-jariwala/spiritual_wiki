I recall Renu ji describing an afternoon in Mussoorie. Gurudev had asked Indu ji — the disciple who oversaw the kitchen — to make sure everyone ate before the excursion: the whole group, Mataji included. Everyone did. When the plates were cleared and only a small quantity of potato subzi remained in the kadai, Renu ji went to ask whether Gurudev should be brought a plate in his room.

He did not wait. He walked into the kitchen himself.

Indu ji lifted the lid to show him what remained — barely enough for one. He said nothing. He picked up a second plate, divided the meal evenly in two, placed one portion before her, and sat down in the kitchen to eat his share. When he finished, he turned to Renu ji and said: *"Putt, jennu khellaan che mazaa agaya na, khaane da mazaa nahin rehnda."* One who learns the pleasure of feeding others, seldom cares about feeding oneself.

*(→ [[stories/gurudev-indu-subzi-mussoorie|Gurudev, Indu ji, and the Last Portion of Subzi]])*

The teaching was in the action, not the sentence. He had not entered the kitchen to make a point — he had entered to eat. The lesson arrived through what he did with the last of the food, and the words followed simply to name it.

**What Barkat Means**

In Gurudev's teaching, *barkat* — the word drawn from the Arabic root for blessing or abundance — means the ordinary grace of having enough: a meal on the table, water in the vessel, shelter overhead. Not wealth. Not accumulation. The unremarkable daily miracle of sufficiency.

It is easy to forget that this is something. Those who have always had enough do not notice what they have. Scarcity is loud; sufficiency is silent. And what is silent can be wasted without noticing.

Barkat is not a right. It is a form of divine favour — received, not earned. And divine favour, mishandled, does not keep flowing. The one who takes it for granted does not lose it dramatically. They lose it quietly, paratha by abandoned paratha.

**The Plate in the Sink**

Some time later, in the family home, a guest left a plate in the kitchen sink — half a paratha, uneaten. Indu didi, who was overseeing the kitchen, had not seen who left it. When Gurudev walked in and found it, he asked her. She did not know, but immediately offered to eat it herself. He picked up the plate before she could reach it and ate the leftovers.

Then he turned to the household:

> *"Learn to respect food. This is barkat — those who have it, waste it. Ask someone less fortunate what a square meal is worth. We must be grateful for what we have."*
>
> — Gurudev, *Witnessing Greatness*, Ch.6, p.54

*(→ [[stories/gurudev-food-barkat-leftover-paratha|Gurudev Eats the Leftover Paratha]])*

He did not name the guest. He did not reprimand anyone directly. He simply stepped ahead of Indu didi before she could take on another person's thoughtlessness. The point was not guilt — it was attention. Something had been given that we were not noticing. The half-eaten paratha was the evidence.

**The Stewardship**

To understand this fully, it helps to see what Gurudev's household looked like from the inside. His home ran on modest means — the kitchen was never lavish, the house was never grand. Mataji quietly ate her children's leftovers so that nothing would be wasted. Visitors were always fed. These were not conspicuous acts of principle. They were simply what the household did: whatever had been received generously was passed on generously.

This is what barkat in practice looks like — not austerity, not renunciation, but a quality of attention. You have enough. You know you have enough. And you live with that knowledge alive, not asleep.

The principle does not stop at food. Time is barkat. The access to a teacher who bothers to train you is barkat. Shakti accumulated through years of practice is barkat. Whatever has been given — if it is treated as entitlement rather than trust — begins, slowly, to diminish. Not through punishment. Simply through the logic of a channel that is blocked from one end.

*"One who learns the pleasure of feeding others, seldom cares about feeding oneself"* — Gurudev said this in a kitchen in Mussoorie over a divided plate of subzi. The inversion is the whole teaching. The moment abundance becomes something to be given rather than kept, you stop worrying about whether you have enough of it. You simply pass it on, and find that the passing replenishes what you gave.

Notice what you have been given. Then notice what you have been wasting. The distance between the two is the practice.
