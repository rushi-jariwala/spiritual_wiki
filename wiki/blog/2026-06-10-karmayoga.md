In 1988, Gurudev was on his knees in an arid plot in Mohammedpur village near Gurgaon — tilling the soil, sowing seeds, planting trees on land that most farmers had already abandoned. Giri ji, one of our gurubhais, had purchased the plot. Gurudev had decided, quietly, without ceremony, that it should be greened. Heavy work. The kind that raises blisters. He did not stop until the land was cultivable again. Neighbouring farmers who had relocated years earlier saw the transformation and returned to their own plots. An entire village came back to life — from one man's effort on one unremarkable afternoon.

*(→ [[stories/gurudev-mohammedpur-greening|Gurudev Greens the Land at Mohammedpur]])*

That is karmayoga. Not a philosophy in the abstract, but a man with dirty hands and a light conscience, expending energy for a purpose that outlasts the act.

**What Karmayoga Actually Means**

The word often gets used as a synonym for doing good — performing one's duty with detachment. That framing is not wrong, but it leaves out the essential dimension. Karmayoga, as Gurudev practised it, is sustained good karma as a *discipline*, done with an awareness of what is at stake: not only the welfare of this life, but the soul's balance sheet across lives. It is the karmic equivalent of compound interest — sown in this incarnation, fructifying across the next several.

The philosopher-king Ashoka understood this precisely. After the devastation of Kalinga, he converted the machinery of an empire into an instrument of *dharma*, and left us a sentence I have returned to many times: *"Work I must for the common weal and the way to achieve is by effort and dispatch; to this end I toil to discharge my debt to human beings and to make some happy; in this world, and the next."* *(→ [[stories/ashoka-kalinga-transformation|Ashoka's Transformation After Kalinga]])* The phrase "in this world and the next" is what separates the karmayogi from the philanthropist. The philanthropist is clearing a present-life account. The karmayogi is investing in a ledger that extends well beyond the body's tenure.

Gurudev put the same logic more plainly at his Khandsa farm: *"I am a man of moderate means. My financial capacity to assist people is limited. I will use my body to serve regardless of how physically demanding the work is."* (*The Guru of Gurus*, p.89) Where money runs short, the body becomes the currency. He ploughed, milked cows, picked vegetables, and assisted at harvest. The work was unglamorous and offered no one a teaching. It offered the soil a future.

**The Sleepless Night**

To understand what sustained this level of commitment, I need to tell you about an evening in the early 1970s.

Gurudev had taken his elder daughter Renu to the market. She asked for two glasses of milk from a sweet shop and drank them with such obvious delight that her father turned to Mataji and asked if there was always enough milk at home. Mataji's reply — that there was never enough money for the children's dietary essentials — stunned him. *(→ [[stories/renu-milk-market-visit|Renu's Milk — The Night of Doubt]])* He hardly slept that night, turning the question over: had he made the right choice, spending himself on others while his own family went short?

The following morning, he was more resolved than ever. Service before self became, from that day, the backbone of everything he built.

What moved me most in that episode was not the resolution — it was the struggle. A man who had been pouring himself into others for years, genuinely asking whether he had his priorities right. He stayed in the doubt through the night and came out the other side not with the doubt suppressed, but with a deeper knowing. That is the difference between spiritual performance and spiritual reality.

**Karmayoga That Outlives the Body**

The deepest confirmation of this path came to me not from Gurudev himself, but from a Parsi gentleman named Mr Marchant in Andheri, Mumbai.

I had been visiting him for treatment during my years with arthritis. He received his prescriptions via planchette from his late guru, never charged a fee, and would not allow his patients to compensate him either. One day I arrived, after Gurudev had cured my arthritis, simply to give Mr Marchant the good news. He set up the planchette as usual. *(→ [[stories/marchant-planchette-healing|Mr Marchant and the Planchette — Post-Death Karma in Action]])* The pendulum swung more aggressively than he had ever seen it. In bewilderment, he turned to the box: "But how can he be alright?" I asked him to ask his guru how I was cured. The pendulum swung again. *"Jungle man, jungle medicine,"* he said. I asked, how much am I cured? "Ninety-five per cent."

His guru, long deceased, was still healing people through him — and still instructing that nothing be charged. That episode strengthened my belief in karmayoga, almost forty years ago. Good karma, done without the need for return, does not stop with the body. The seva was the cashmere; the goals were already knit.

**The Economics of Karmayoga**

Understanding the logic behind this daily expenditure of energy requires engaging with what Gurudev called the basic economics of spiritual life. His teaching on the subject was spare and exact: *"Luxury is an expense in spiritualism."* (*The Guru of Gurus*, p.116)

He chose a hard life — not as mortification, but as energy conservation. Every comfort diverts the shakti that could otherwise be directed toward service. Every luxury is a karmic purchase drawn from funds that could have remained available for acts whose returns compound across lifetimes. The Mohammedpur field, the blistered hands, the long days — none of this was theatre. It was the application of a clear-eyed calculation about where to spend oneself.

*Karma Sutra* states the same principle from another angle: *"Today's fortune is yesterday's effort; so today, we have an opportunity to sow the seeds for the next crop, while we enjoy the present one."* (*Karma Sutra*, Ch.1, p.7) The crop analogy is simple. The farming is not.

Tilling begins long before the harvest — and if done right, it continues long after this life is over.
