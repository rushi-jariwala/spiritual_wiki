# Barkat (Divine Abundance)

## Definition

**Barkat** — translated literally as abundance or blessing — refers specifically to the *luck of making one's resources last longer*. It is the grace that stretches what you have: a meal that feeds more than expected, a salary that covers more than it should, a surplus that persists without obvious cause.

Gurudev used the term in a specific moral context: barkat is not merely a condition — it is a responsibility.

## Core Teaching

Gurudev drew the distinction sharply when he came across a half-eaten paratha left in the kitchen sink. After eating the leftovers himself, he addressed his disciples:

> "Learn to respect food. This is barkat — those who have it, waste it. Ask someone less fortunate what a square meal is worth. We must be grateful for what we have."
> — Gurudev, *Witnessing Greatness*, Ch.6, p.81

The teaching has two movements:
1. **Gratitude**: to have regular, sufficient food is a form of grace that most of the world does not share. Treating it as ordinary is a form of ingratitude.
2. **Non-waste as reverence**: wasting what has been given is not merely careless — it is a disrespect of the barkat itself. In karmic terms, it is spending spiritual credit carelessly.

## Nuances

- The same logic extends beyond food: time, money, health, relationships — any resource carries its own barkat. Those who "have it, waste it" are identified not as malicious but as unconscious.
- Barkat is implicitly linked to [[concepts/positive-karma]]: the abundance that arrives as a result of past good deeds. Wasting it is a form of disrespect toward the karma that produced it.
- Gurudev modelled this not through restriction but through attention. He added heaps of white butter to parathas served to visitors, yet insisted nothing be left uneaten. The generosity and the reverence were not in tension — both expressed the same consciousness toward what is given.

## In Practice

The unwritten rule at Gurudev's home: no one leaves food on their plate unfinished. Mataji, faced with fussy-eating children, would quietly eat their leftovers. Gurudev himself ate a guest's half-eaten paratha before Indu didi could react.

The practice is not about frugality for its own sake. It is about maintaining the connection between receiving and recognising — closing the gap of unconsciousness that sits between having plenty and knowing what it means.

> [!tip] Barkat is the householder's version of the teaching on [[concepts/vairagya]] within abundance: you can live fully and generously *without* the carelessness that comes from taking resources for granted. See [[concepts/grihasth-ashram]].
