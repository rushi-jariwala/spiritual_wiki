# Customised Guidance

> *"The guru has the freedom to change rules, customise them for each disciple, and even use backhand methods of tutoring."*
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.11, p.102

## Definition

Customised guidance is the defining difference between a guru-disciple relationship and any other form of spiritual instruction. Where standard religious formats — Sikhism, Christianity, and most traditions — ask all followers to implement the same collection of teachings, the guru works with each disciple as an individual. The guru reads the disciple's destiny, planetary movements, psychological makeup, social background, and spiritual competency, then designs a unique playing field for each one.

> "Unlike standard format education that feeds the same curriculum to all students, thereby producing a generation of mediocrity, a guru's teachings are more like a personalised and customised tuition."
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.11, p.126

This is only possible if the guru is physically present in the disciple's life. His physical presence is therefore not a luxury but a requirement for this level of training.

## Raising a Disciple

Guiding a disciple is compared to raising a child — except Guruism requires more than just understanding the disciple, making the process far more complicated. The guru must:

- Recognise the disciple's flaws and shortcomings
- Understand what makes the disciple's mind tick
- Account intuitively for the disciple's destiny and planetary movements
- Sense the troughs and crests on the spiritual path ahead
- Know when to accelerate and when to let the disciple run at a slower pace

The guru can also sense when an evolved disciple is growing despondent about his progress. Convincing him — through experience — that he has indeed moved forward is part of the curriculum.

> *Gurudev's guidance to Hingori's wife on faith:*
> "You have to walk into a dark tunnel and carry on treading relentlessly, even if you cannot see a light. This will continue, until somewhere in the far distance, you see a faint light. As you get closer, the light will seem to get brighter. The journey is long, dark and deep. It is a marathon you need to run and the pleasure can come only after the endeavour."
> — Gurudev, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.11, p.102

### On Spiritual Stagnancy

Hingori describes a decade of spiritual stagnancy he passed through after Gurudev had already died. During this phase he continued seva but neither meditated nor engaged in spiritual conversation. He credits Gurudev's earlier teaching — remembered at the right moment — as what kept him on the path:

> "If one moves too quickly, their train is bound to get stranded at some station and they will have to wait until it is time to move again. One will only reach their destination when they are meant to and therefore, speed and enthusiasm are of no help. What helps is consistency of intention and the decision to walk continuously in that direction."
> — Gurudev, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.11, p.103

Hingori notes that had Gurudev been alive, he would likely have shortened the sabbatical by assigning the training of others — keeping the disciple active even when personal practice had stalled.

## Gurudev's Range of Disciples

Gurudev's disciples came from every social stratum: metropolitan professionals, villagers from Punjab and Himachal Pradesh, the barely literate, and the barely solvent. He invented ways to make each one self-sufficient and effective within their own context. See [[stories/mamaji-vedji-sthan]] for the Mamaji-Vedji collaboration in Jalandhar: a paan shop owner and an affluent businessman each assigned roles that made a sthan function successfully for over two decades.

The four types of spiritual labour at a sthan illustrate customisation in practice: administrative disciples, healers who occupy the gaddi, those who cook prasad, those who manage patient queues. Each type of service earns spiritual credits differently.

## The Four Training Segments

The guru's training method for a disciple normally includes four segments:

### 1. Physical Discipline

Fitness and flexibility support meditation and seva. The guru may prescribe yoga, pranayam, fasting on certain days, and restricted foods. Fasting serves multiple purposes:

- **Sense conquest** — the sense of taste is a downer on the spiritual path; indulging it excessively hinders sadhana
- **Digestive clearing** — fasting gives the body the opportunity to exhaust undigested stocks of food that otherwise remain as physical and karmic ballast
- **Karmic non-obligation** — by mentally assigning one's foregone food to someone who needs it more, the disciple uses the power of intent to benefit another without creating a debt
- **Avoiding negative-energy food** — food cooked in homes affected by negative energy can infect the consumer; food paid for by another can create karmic obligation

The five senses — especially touch and hearing — have been "a failure for thousands of spiritual aspirants." Gurudev's approach was balance over abstinence: he enjoyed music but did not allow it to become an obsession. Hingori learned to restrict music to his car and to give up colognes, perfumes, and the need for scenic environments.

> Golden Principle: "If you continue to sustain a crusade against your senses, it does not matter if the results are not a 100%. What matters is the intent and effort of this discipline."
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.11, p.106

### 2. Mental Tuning

Spiritual evolution is, in the view of almost every spiritualist, about the mind. The five forces that pull a disciple down in what Hingori calls the "snakes and ladders game of self-evolution" are: **kama** (sensuality), **krodh** (anger/reactivity), **lobh** (greed), **moh** (attachment), and **ahamkaar** (ego / the sense of 'I-ness'). See [[concepts/kleshas]].

When 'I-ness' comes into play, it makes a person feel as small as the identities he has created — far smaller than what it would mean to identify with the expansiveness of the aatma.

The guru's aim is to ensure his disciples master meditation. Calmness and control are the *sine qua non* for meditation and mantra practice; distractions must be reined in. Dealing with thoughts — overcoming them without being subsumed — is a core technique. Since billions of neurons keep the mind perpetually active, equanimity is hard but necessary; mental energy must be economised.

The kleshas of attraction, aversion, ego, ignorance, and fear are reactions to almost every situation. Attachment to family — the most common — is "a weakness that keeps you bound not only during this life, but also in the afterlife. It does not allow your spirit to progress, as it is still trying to stay connected to its family and worldly desires."

> Golden Principle: "One must feel attached to none but connected to all."
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.11, p.108

Gurudev demonstrated fearlessness in Amritsar: finding a huge snake in the path to his daily meditation spot, his desire to meditate was greater than the threat of being bitten. He stepped across the snake to reach the tree.

The peak mental aspiration — one the guru must help the disciple reach — is **self-worship**: worshipping not merely the physical, astral, or causal being, but the jivaatma as a complete package. See [[concepts/self-worship]].

### 3. Emotional Training

Emotional stability is a prerequisite for spiritual apprenticeship. The guru teaches emotional equilibrium — the ability to balance at the **centre of the seesaw** rather than sit at either end. This is a state of neutrality that is nearly impossible to achieve since kleshas keep pulling in one direction or the other.

Emotions to be reduced: anger, selfish love, excitement and joy, dislikes, hatred, depression-born complexes. These are "downers to a spiritualist's progress" and downers to everyone's progress.

The Eightfold Path of Patanjali (*Yoga Sutras*) — silencing the fluctuations of the mind — leads to balanced emotions and enhanced focus on the inner self.

### 4. Spiritual Training

Proficiency in yoga, fasting, and service are necessary but not sufficient. The guru's magic ingredient is **gyan** — inner, often intuitive knowledge. Few are born with functioning intuition; most attain it through intense meditation, mantra recitation, seva, and bhakti. The result is intuitive hearing and foresight, understood as the opening of the **Ajna Chakra** (third eye). In tantric philosophy, this occurs when the kundalini rises from its base to the Ajna Chakra.

Hingori describes his own third eye beginning to open — visions of the future, an inner voice — but Gurudev deliberately arrested the process. He placed his hand on Hingori's head and ran it down his spine, lowering the kundalini. Hingori felt depressed. Gurudev explained: "I did not want me to get stuck at the forehead chakra. I felt you might stagnate at the level of the third eye, enjoying your status as a psychic... The temptation of showing off could have been a threat to progress of the self." See [[stories/hingori-third-eye-opened-closed]].

The story of Ramakrishna Paramahansa under his guru Totapari is offered as an illustration: Totapari recognised that Ramakrishna's intense devotion to Goddess Kali — however genuine — was an obstacle to enlightenment. Conversations and counselling failed. Totapari finally used a piece of broken glass to scratch Ramakrishna's forehead during a trance, enabling him to conjure a sword and shred the image of Kali. Liberated from devotion, Ramakrishna attained Paramahansa status. The moral: a person moves from emotions to emotionlessness, from devotee to disciple. See [[stories/ramakrishna-totapari-kali]].

One of the greatest curses of spiritual practice is **delusion of the mind**. The disciple must comprehend that this world in all its manifestations is a projection of the cosmic mind — maya. He must understand destiny, the laws of karma, and ultimately that he is above karma: not the doer of any deed, but an observer of destiny. See [[concepts/maya]], [[concepts/non-doership]].

## The Guru's Guiding Principle

> Golden Principle: "The guru must lead the disciple,
> from passion to dispassion
> from attachment to detachment
> from discontentment to contentment
> from ignorance to knowledge"
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.11, p.113

"Just four lines but it could take more than four lifetimes to fulfil."
