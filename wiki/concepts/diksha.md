# Diksha

## Definition

**Diksha** is the commitment of acceptance given by a guru to his disciple. By granting diksha, the guru holds himself responsible for the disciple's spiritual growth and enlightenment — a responsibility that may extend beyond the present lifetime into multiple lives.

> Diksha is the commitment of acceptance given by a guru to his disciple. The guru works towards the disciple's enlightenment and spiritual attainment holding himself responsible for his disciple's growth.
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.10, p.95

This is not a casual relationship. Before making such a commitment, both sides must find congruency. The guru must intuitively assess not only the disciple's current capability but also his long-term potential — because if a disciple loses interest or abandons the path halfway, all the guru's efforts are a wash.

## Forms of Diksha

### Monastic (Ceremonial)

In the monastic order, diksha is given ceremoniously. The monk shaves his head, changes into robes, and receives a new name from the guru. The intent is to mark a clean break — end of the old life, start of the new. The visible change (different appearance) serves two functions: recognition by others as a spiritual being, and self-recognition of having made the change.

In most spiritual orders, diksha is accompanied by a **mantra**, also called **naam**, which forms the operative channel of the relationship.

### Householder (Informal)

Gurudev did not believe in the demonstration of spiritualism. He held that people connected to him should blend in, not stand out. His way of giving diksha was so informal that a disciple "did not even realise you were part of his inner circle."

> There are no hard and fast rules for diksha. Different spiritual orders follow different systems.
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.10, p.96

Gurudev gave diksha mostly to those who were householders, following the example of **Guru Nanak Dev** — who was himself a householder. See [[concepts/grihasth-ashram]].

## Hingori's Own Diksha (Personal Testimony)

> When I first met Gurudev, it was for all the wrong reasons. I had no clue that one day this great master would give me diksha. Besides, I had no interest in spiritualism. I thought it was meant for the 'saffron-robed guys'… If somebody had asked me then whether I would like to receive diksha, I would probably have doubled up with laughter! Since destiny had to fulfil itself, it obviously ignored my interpretation of diksha and my attitude towards it.
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.10, p.95–96

This testimony places diksha squarely within the framework of [[concepts/destiny]]: the disciple does not choose diksha so much as destiny arranges for it. Uncommitted or even dismissive attitudes are no barrier when the spiritual contract has already been written.

## Benefits of Diksha — Mutual

A critical teaching: the benefit of diksha is not one-sided.

**For the disciple**: access to the guru's long-term commitment across lifetimes; guided evolution; activation of spiritual potential.

**For the guru**: the ability to multiply himself — to turn one tree into an orchard. With more siddh disciples, the guru's capacity to serve the greater good expands far beyond what he could accomplish alone. See [[wiki/analogies/analogies]] — *Tree into Orchard* analogy.

> Hence, great disciples do greater gurus make!
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.10, p.97

This principle is borne out historically:
- **Jesus's disciples** spread Christianity across the globe, often at the cost of their lives — martyred by uncivilised tribespeople who resisted conversion.
- **Guru Machindranath, Ramakrishna, Yukteshwar Giri, Guru Nanak Dev**, and the other Sikh gurus all had highly evolved disciples who took forward their work with efficacy and zeal.

## Householder Diksha and Faster Attainment

Gurudev paradoxically taught that householder disciples achieve *faster* spiritual attainment than those in the monastic/ascetic order:

> Due to the myriad difficulties of being a householder (viz. earning a livelihood, performing social duties and involvement in commercial ventures), practicing spiritualism enabled quicker spiritual attainment.
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.10, p.96

The logic: the spiritual progress of a householder is more pronounced because of the "angle of difficulty" — navigating the constant pressures of ordinary life while maintaining practice. When **seva** is added to the system, the acceleration is even more pronounced.

The practical evidence: most of Gurudev's disciples could become **siddh in the Mahagayatri Mantra** in three to four years, while practitioners in formal settings typically take two or three decades even to attain the abridged **Gayatri Mantra**. See [[practices/seva]], [[concepts/grihasth-ashram]].

## "Diksha Not Diksha" — False Commitment

Not all diksha carries genuine commitment.

> As a system, diksha is very easy to exploit, and there are those who get hoodwinked into following frivolous gurus. Though, I do believe this too is a matter of destiny.
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.10, p.97

Commercially motivated gurus offer diksha to those who supply material comforts. Because no genuine long-term bond forms, the relationship remains superficial and temporary — spiritually worthless for the disciple. In ancient times, diksha and the guru-disciple relationship were taken with the utmost seriousness. Today, Hingori laments, it has "to some extent become a fad."

> [!warning] Committing to a non-siddh guru results in a wasted and misguided spiritual journey. Hingori flags this as "more a subject of fate than free choice" — consistent with the broader destiny framework of the Hingori Sutras.
