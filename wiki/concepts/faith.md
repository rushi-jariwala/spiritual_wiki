# Faith

> *"A disciple's faith binds the guru to him."*
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.8, p.69

## Definition

In the Hingori framework, faith is not a sentiment or a religious attitude — it is the binding force that creates an obligatory relationship between a disciple and their guru. Faith is intangible, yet operationally precise: its depth determines what the guru is required to deliver to the disciple.

> Golden Principle: Sometimes, blind faith in a siddh guru can help one see better over time.
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.8, p.69

## Core Teaching

### Faith as the Binding Mechanism

Faith obliges the guru. When a disciple crosses sufficient boundaries to develop genuine faith, the guru — in a manner unsaid — registers this and feels compelled to offer all he can as a mentor. The mission of the guru is to make the disciple succeed despite all odds, and it is during this process that a stronger and deeper connection develops.

> Golden Principle: Unshakeable faith in your guru steers him to an obligatory stance, binding him to your evolution. Therein lies a winning formula!
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.8, p.73

### Faith Enables Dual Connection

Faith enables the disciple's spirit and physical being to develop separate relationships with the guru's spirit and physical being — reaching even to the level of the guru's *jivaatma* (individualised consciousness supreme).

The disciple's spirit recognises that the guru's spirit, being more advanced, oscillates at all levels of consciousness and can therefore be beneficial at multiple levels — advising, guiding, and directing in ways that come "in the language of a PhD rather than the garble of grade V."

Because the disciple's faith is unquestionable, all arguments and doubts within the mind are set aside, and the disciple follows a singular diktat — that of the guru's mind. This is the magic of faith.

### Faith as a Wonder Drug

Hingori's personal testimony: though Gurudev consistently told him he had a great deal of inner knowledge (*gyan*), Hingori had no religious background and assumed he knew nothing about spiritualism. He did not even know the names of Indian gods and goddesses. He acted on a guru-bhai's lesson to "believe Gurudev blindly."

> For me, faith worked like a wonder drug.
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.8, p.82

Knowledge and concepts gradually percolated and became clear. Believing the unbelievable became easy. Faith made progress possible where reason alone would have blocked it.

## Testing Faith

A disciple surrendering to the guru does not automatically establish a two-way relationship — it is only a one-sided appeal that awaits acceptance. The guru tests the disciple's faith at various stages and on various counts, sometimes misguiding him or failing to fulfil an expectation, specifically to ascertain whether the faith is genuine or conditional.

If faith is found genuine — even partially — the guru tries to improve the disciple's capability. If he cannot succeed, he does not accept the relationship.

The property negotiation story illustrates this: Gurudev sent multiple disciples to buy a property, expecting none to succeed. When Hingori went against explicit instructions and overpaid for the property, he succeeded — and Gurudev was amused rather than annoyed. The test was not the negotiation; it was the willingness to go off the beaten track in service of the sthan's larger purpose. See [[stories/guru-sutra-ch8-property-test]].

## Faith Needs to Be Exclusive to One Guru

Hingori is explicit: faith must be directed at a single guru for the relationship to function at 100%.

> When a person reveres two gurus, there is a tendency for each master to step back and allow the other to take over. Thus, the disciple suffers a lack of commitment from either of them.
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.8, p.73

During a visit to Canada, Hingori noticed that devotees who kept photographs of multiple spiritual seers had unresolved impediments in their personal lives. He told them to devote to just one and remove the rest.

> [!tip] Hingori acknowledges exceptions: some spiritual gurus willingly send their disciples to other gurus for specific training. Gurudev himself sent Ravi Trehan to the Yogananda Mission for training in Dhyana Yoga. And many people find their way to the sthans after being guided by visions of Shirdi Sai Baba and Muktananda. These cases are guru-directed, not disciple-initiated plurality.

> [!warning] Tension: Ch.8 states faith must be exclusive to one guru for full commitment to operate. Yet Ch.8 also acknowledges cases where gurus send disciples for training with other gurus, and where historical figures like The Buddha had multiple teachers. Hingori frames these as exceptional and often guru-sanctioned rather than disciple-driven. But the line is not perfectly drawn.

## When Faith Is Shaken

If a disciple's faith in his guru is shaken, the guru continues to serve the disciple — though no longer obligated. The relationship changes from guru-and-disciple to that of a spiritually accomplished person and a seeker. The guru will help as best he can, leaving the rest to the disciple's destiny.

> Golden Principle: Faith is the bridge that helps you cross over from your physical path to your spiritual future.
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.8, p.75

## In Practice

- Believe the guru even when his statements make no immediate sense — this is not credulity but the opening of a channel through which inner knowledge can arrive.
- Do not fragment faith across multiple gurus; the resulting dilution means neither guru takes full responsibility.
- When tested — even misguided — do not abandon faith. The test is precisely designed to distinguish genuine faith from conditional faith.
- Faith's development takes time due to the complexity of the mind, but once it works, it reveals aspects of spiritualism that make progress far easier.

## *Guru Sutra* Ch.19 — Faith, Entitlement, and Losing Faith at a Sthan

### Entitlement vs. Faith

Ch.19 introduces a sharp distinction: a disciple who expects faith to be *rewarded* is still in the stage of a child or employee, not a disciple.

> Till such time that people have a sense of entitlement, they are playing the role of children and not disciples. Children or pets or employees can be accepted with a sense of entitlement because it is common nature and not superior nature.
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.19, p.198

A disciple must look at faith not as an input for reward but as grace — the grace that enables deep faith in the guru. The moment a disciple expects a return, he remains a *disciple-in-the-making*.

### Losing Faith at a Sthan

When a regular visitor to a sthan loses faith, their connection weakens — and with it, the sthan's influence on their spiritual growth:

> There have been examples of devotees losing faith and then regaining it, either months or years later. In such cases of lost and found, the spiritual growth of the devotees remains suspended during the phase of no-faith, even though they may continue to have spiritual experiences.
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.19, p.193

Spiritual experiences may continue during the no-faith phase, but **evolution plateaus**. This is not framed as failure — but as a suspension requiring re-engagement to resume the climb.

## The Multi-Lifetime Dimension of Faith (*Witnessing Greatness*, Ch.4)

*Witnessing Greatness* Ch.4 adds a dimension to faith not present in the *Guru Sutra* treatment: faith across incarnations. The disciple-guru bond is not created in this lifetime — it is resumed. Santlal ji's nine previous incarnations with Gurudev, Puran ji's Ujjain and Guru Nanak Dev era connections, Ravi ji's vision of the Shahjahanpur mansion — all suggest that the faith a disciple feels upon meeting the guru is not new. It is the activation of a bond formed centuries prior.

The scepticism of Mumbaikars, including Hingori himself (five years of delay after the arthritis cure), is thus reframed: not as lack of faith but as the expected friction of a bond not yet fully reactivated. The gate opens, but the disciple must step through.
