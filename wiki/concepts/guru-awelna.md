# Guru Awelna

## Definition

There is no specific translation for *guru awelna*. Its meaning ranges from not following the instructions of one's guru, to upsetting, insulting, or hurting his sentiments, to being wrongful or deceitful. Although by nature gurus are very forgiving, guru awelna seldom goes unpunished. The person committing the act suffers a lot more than bargained for.

## The Mechanism of Punishment

The punishment does not come from the guru's hand but from within:

> The inner consciousness of a person punishes its conscious part, to square off its samskars of extreme guilt and nullify the dark impression of itself by making it suffer. It is my belief that the spirit within does not easily forgive its outer self for having wronged the guru (the very icon of its ambitions).
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.12, p.222

The guru, by his nature, forgives. It is the disciple's own spirit that cannot forgive the outer self for having wronged the one being it most admires. The punishment is thus self-administered at the level of samskars, not externally inflicted.

## Awelna vs. Disagreement

Hingori draws a sharp distinction between awelna and legitimate disagreement:

> You can confess to your guru that you have a different approach and a separate way of looking at the subject at hand, thus taking his permission to hold a different viewpoint. An advanced guru will accept that every chemistry has its own equation and each cannot be congruent to the other.
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.12, p.125

> Obedience to a guru is the strength of discipleship.
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.12, p.125

Awelna is not intellectual difference but a moral failure: deception, disobedience, arrogance, or wilful disregard for the guru's instruction. A difference of opinion, openly declared and permission-sought, is not awelna.

## Stories of Guru Awelna

### Hingori's Lie About Nagesh

Hingori gave money to Gurudev's cook Nagesh despite Gurudev having forbidden Nagesh from collecting money from others. When Gurudev questioned him, Hingori lied to protect Nagesh. Days later he twisted his foot in a ditch and was in agony for three days. When Gurudev called him over and asked again, Hingori finally confessed. Gurudev's words: *"You must never lie to your guru. Never!"* He then asked a senior disciple to cure the sprain — and the pain vanished immediately. See [[stories/hingori-nagesh-lie-gurudev]].

### Satte's Paralysis Returns

Satte came to Gurudev paralysed from the hip downwards. After months of treatment, he could walk and drive. He took permission to go home for a few days but was instructed to return on the day of a solar eclipse. Satte got lazy and delayed. The paralysis returned. He apologised; Gurudev kept him at the sthan for years, but Satte never fully recovered. Another healer refused to treat him, recognising him as the devotee of a very powerful saint. Gurudev treated him affectionately, but the original level of healing was never restored. See [[stories/satte-paralysis-disobedience]].

### Rajpalji's Sore Throat

Rajpalji sat at the sthan singing songs with the intent of impressing visitors. Gurudev looked at him and said, "Alright son, let me see how you sing again." Within no time, Rajpalji's throat became sore and remained that way for over a year. During a visit to Shimla with Gurudev, he pleaded for a cure. Gurudev asked him to pick snow off the ground and eat it. Rajpalji complied and his throat was instantly normal again. See [[stories/rajpal-hoarse-voice-snow]].

### Bade Jainsahib — The Worst Case

Bade Jainsahib was among Gurudev's first disciples, progressing faster than any other — healing over the telephone, bearing spiritual symbols in his hand. He had extraordinary experiences, including a six-foot being emerging from a Shankar statue and claiming to be a manifestation of Shiv. The speed of his spiritual growth became a handicap: his ego went beyond control. He threatened to visit Gurgaon and defeat Gurudev in spiritual combat. That day, he lost control of his mind and was diagnosed mentally imbalanced. Gurudev eventually forgave him and took him back, but Bade Jainsahib was never the same again. See [[stories/bade-jainsahib-guru-awelna]].

> The glass was rejoined but the cracks were clearly visible.
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.12, p.124

Hingori notes that Gurudev mentioned Bade Jainsahib was manipulated by a powerful force — possibly the same being that had appeared out of the statue. The awelna lay in the fact that he gave in to such an influence and was attracted by it.

## A Warning on Spiritual Power

The Bade Jainsahib story leads to a teaching that goes beyond awelna:

> Although, being all-powerful seems like an attainment, there is always a thin dividing line between using the attainment valuably and being consumed by it. You are all-powerful is in a deeper way identifying with the aatma, which is all powerful as it is a part of the consciousness supreme. But believing so only because 'you' may have had a vision of a being who claimed to be a manifestation of shiv, or because 'your' guru gave 'you' power to heal others, is crossing the line. Clearly it is delusionary to believe it is 'you' as an independent identity that has attained the power.
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.12, pp.124–125

Identifying with the consciousness supreme is the highest attainment. Identifying with the physical or astral self inflated by spiritual experiences is the trap. Spiritual power must be received with humility and grace, not allowed to inflate one's ego.
