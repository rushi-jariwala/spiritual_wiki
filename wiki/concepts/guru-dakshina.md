# Guru Dakshina

## Definition

*Guru dakshina* is the offering made by a student to a teacher in repayment of the karmic debt incurred through receiving teaching. It is the formal acknowledgment that knowledge received creates an obligation — and that the obligation must be honoured.

> Tann, mann, dhann or the body, mind and wealth are an offering the student makes to his preceptor as guru dakshina or repayment to guru.
> — Hingori, *Karma Sutra*, Ch.2, p.39

## The Three Forms

| Sanskrit | Meaning | Nature of Offering |
|---|---|---|
| **Tann** | Body | Physical service, presence |
| **Mann** | Mind | Attention, devotion, learning, carrying the teaching |
| **Dhann** | Wealth | Material offerings, financial support |

All three together constitute the fullest repayment. Each alone is partial.

## Why the Teacher's Debt is Special

Among the six categories of [[concepts/karmic-debt|karmic debt]], the guru's is the heaviest in one specific sense:

> The spiritual teacher gets the cream of the credit because he/she helps you first unlearn all the delusions and cleanses your slate before he/she can rewrite on it. The understanding of karma theory and practicing it at best is the result of his/her performance. It is an invaluable debt and cannot be measured.
> — *Karma Sutra*, Ch.2, p.39

The debt to a spiritual guru is therefore *qualitatively* different from the debt to a school teacher or a parent — it is the debt for being able to perceive reality correctly at all.

## Traditional and Extreme Examples

Hingori cites these not as recommendations but as illustrations of the *weight* of the concept:

- **Eklavya** — Guru Dronacharya asked him to repay by cutting off his right thumb. Eklavya complied. The thumb was his greatest skill — the sacrifice matched the debt.
- **Ravan** — Offered his head to his guru, Lord Shiva, as guru dakshina (whether literally or symbolically is left open).

> These are not recommendations but only bizarre and unique examples to stress a point.
> — *Karma Sutra*, Ch.2, p.39

Conventional forms of dakshina include: coconut offerings; 11 sets of clothes; utilities like bed and umbrella.

## The Onward Transmission

In the Hingori Sutras framework, the most complete form of dakshina is not a material offering but **passing the teaching forward**:

> The only way to neutralise that debt is to work hard towards being a good student to the satisfaction of your guru. Thereafter, spreading wisdom down the line and crediting that karma to your guru is an act of repayment.
> — *Karma Sutra*, Ch.2, p.39

This is precisely the model Hingori himself follows — [[entities/hingori|Hingori]] transmits Gurudev's teachings and abilities to a new generation, and credits the karma to [[entities/mahaguru|Gurudev]].

## Guru Sutra Ch.12 — Historical Context and Four Forms

*Guru Sutra* Ch.12 expands the concept substantially, placing it in its historical context and offering four specific forms of repayment.

In ancient times, spiritual masters housed and looked after their disciples in their own homes or ashrams. When the training period was over, the disciple offered a material token of gratitude — dakshina — usually a small sum of money meant for the guru's sustenance and not extravagance, enabling him to live comfortably and continue mentoring others rather than investing time in earning his keep.

> Of course, every practice can be, and is often abused. There are many spiritual gurus who charge money from those they mentor. Certain cults demand large donations turning the relationship commercial, wherein the guru becomes the bought!
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.12, p.118

### Tangible and Intangible Dakshina

Guru dakshina can be tangible, intangible, or both. While it can be given as material wealth, it can also be given as the carrying-on of the guru's *parampara* (tradition), spreading his words and deeds to countless others, and serving and helping people in the guru's name.

The disciples of Jesus built churches, inspired people to follow him, and converted Christianity into the religion with the largest following to this day — a form of guru dakshina. Buddhist monasteries and Sikh gurudwaras are examples of edifices built as memorial dakshina.

### Four Practical Forms (Guru Sutra)

1. **Feed, educate, and pay for the health and well-being of people**, attributing these deeds to your guru as an arpan (offering).

2. **Complete some of the work your guru left incomplete** while in his physical form. Hingori cites seva as Gurudev's intent: convincing people about the presence of divinity not just in theory but in personal experience. The sthan's disciples continue that legacy.

3. **Spread the message of your guru's philosophy and teachings** to people who do not know him — irrespective of whether the guru is alive or not.

4. **Lead people to your guru or his samadhi**, and if they benefit — through healing or problem-solving — you too are considered a beneficiary in the interaction.

> Throughout history, all saints though made of flesh and blood have performed superhuman feats. Similarly, Gurudev's disciples look like people-next-door but would qualify as saints; both in power and attitude.
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.12, p.119

### A Missed Opportunity as Cautionary Note

Hingori narrates two instances of not acting on visions where people in trouble appeared to him — Riaz (a college friend who later developed muscular sclerosis) and a former prime minister (who was later assassinated). A guru bhai had the same vision of the prime minister at approximately the same time. Hingori reflects that had they acted, they might have extended Gurudev's grace to these individuals — an unexplored form of dakshina, now permanently missed.
