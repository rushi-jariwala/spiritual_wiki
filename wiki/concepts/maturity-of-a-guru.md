# Maturity of a Guru

## Definition

A guru matures with time and experience, passing through recognisable phases just as his disciples do. Hingori writes from lived experience: "I have observed myself mature as a guru, just like I once saw myself mature as a disciple. Both were painful processes." The six qualities developed through this maturing — forgiveness, patience, humility, tactfulness, collaboration, and role-play — are the practical curriculum of guruhood, distinct from those needed to be a good disciple.

> A guru during his lifetime goes through the process of maturing with time and experience. Therefore, he must have empathy for his disciples during the various phases of their evolution as well.
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.15, p.159

## Core Teaching

The transition from excellent disciple to effective guru is not automatic. Not every good actor makes a good director. Several new qualities must be cultivated:

### Forgiveness

The scale of a guru's forgiveness is different from anything a disciple encounters. Gurudev had hundreds of disciples and their downlines — "for him, forgiveness was a wholesale business. Without forgiveness, he would have had to contend with an extensive list of people to fail, suspend and probably eliminate from his core group."

Hingori admits his own early failure here: when disciples made the same mistakes he once made, he could not forgive easily. He expected more from them than he had expected from himself at their stage. He terms this "unfair but true."

Key cases of his own forgiveness struggles:

- A sevadaar who enjoyed being a healer and let pride inflate his ego was suspended for a year; his guru bhais eventually pleaded his case and he was reinstated. *"If movie stars can have inflated egos due to their popularity, it is quite possible for a spiritualist to let his perceived godliness get to his head."*
- Singh, a sevadaar who asked for help giving up drinking, was deliberately not aided for two years. Hingori felt Singh needed to find the corrective strength within himself. Singh eventually became a teetotaller unaided.
- Fault-finding among disciples, competitive posturing, accepting favours from visitors — each of these made forgiveness difficult, and Hingori held some disciples back from promotion until they corrected these patterns.

> At a conscious level, I have become more empathetic towards the erring disciples. At the subconscious level, I see some of them being punished and receiving setbacks. I realise the energy we have access to is a double-edged sword and can sometimes hurt the hand that wields it.
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.15, p.212

### Patience

Kabir's doha captures this quality precisely:

> "Dheere dheere re mana, dheere sab kuch hoye
> Mali seenche so ghara, ritu aaye phal hoye"
> (Everything takes time and happens at its own pace. Even while the gardener may water the plant every day, the fruit only appears in the flowering season.)
> — Kabir, cited in *Guru Sutra*, Ch.15, p.221

Gurudev's version: accept your spiritual journey as it is, what it is, when it is — because only at the appointed time will it reach fruition. Hingori's personal lesson came through a decade of stagnation after Gurudev's demise. He continued seva but had no initiative for much else and felt disconnected from disciples and spiritual friends. "It took a lot to teach me patience."

As guru, one must not expect disciples to succeed before their destined time. Many gurus err by expecting regimented precision from householders who carry the weight of family, work, and societal obligations — "amidst all this, finding time for being a good disciple is no meagre task!"

### Humility

Spiritual humility, as Hingori defines it, is not a manner of behaviour — not soft-spokenness or meekness. It is the inner realisation that all beings carry the same divinity at their core.

> When you go deeper into the meaning of humility, you realise that all beings and life forms have the same divinity at the core, just as you do. True spiritual humility lies in this realisation.
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.15, p.166

Being on a high pedestal one did not crave but cannot avoid is not pride — it is a consequence of others' recognition. The question the spiritualist must ask: "Do you see yourself as superior because people who are actually not lesser than you, consider you a master? Or do you treat people with empathy and look at them with gratitude for having given you a high pedestal?"

The Indian scriptural formulation of ultimate humility is *Aham Brahmasmi* — "I am Brahman, I am the consciousness supreme." Believing you are part of the consciousness supreme is not spiritual vanity; it is the realisation that *all* beings are themselves the consciousness supreme. This is spiritual humility in its ultimate form.

The classical illustration is the encounter between [[entities/adi-shankaracharya|Adi Shankarcharya]] and the social outcast at the River Ganges. When Shankarcharya's disciples ordered a hunter to move aside, the hunter challenged him: if non-dual Brahman is the only reality, how has the sense of difference overtaken you? Shankarcharya's response: "A person who sees the entire world as aatma only, whose mind is unshakeably established in that conviction is worthy of worship, irrespective of who he is in worldly ways." See [[stories/shankaracharya-hunter-ganges]].

A guru must also guard against competing with the egos of his disciples. When a disciple makes a disrespectful remark, the guru must pass it off in the moment and admonish later. "A guru must understand that for a disciple to come up to his level of realisation, the journey is a long and difficult one."

### Tactfulness

Tactfulness is the quality that allows a guru to lead disciples through spiritual attainment without heavy-handedness. Gurudev exemplified it through studied innocence — he would ask questions pretending not to know the answers, listen attentively to disciples' suggestions as if they were pearls of wisdom, and then act on his own prior judgment.

> "While people thought of Gurudev as Bholenath (the innocent one), I found him to be Chaturnath (the clever one)."
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.15, p.170

Key distinction: a *saint* does not need to be shrewd because he only has his own evolution to consider. A *guru* must be shrewd because he manages the spiritual evolution of many people across generations.

Gurudev's tactfulness also extended to handling disciples' misconduct with indirect measures: a wallet thief was not hauled up, but informed that his seva benefits would be revoked. "He passed it off saying that the miscreant would pay for it, with the benefit of his seva being revoked."

See [[stories/gurudev-cowshed-tactfulness]] and [[stories/malhotraji-luggage-resignation]] for two extended examples.

### Collaboration

Gurudev fostered cross-tradition spiritual alliances, telling his disciples they had connections with Khwaja Moinuddin Chishti, Devi Renuka and Parshuram, Shirdi Sai Baba, Augarh, Baba Makhdoom, Guru Nanak Dev, and Guru Gobind Singh — and that they could request help from any of them.

The principle extends beyond inter-tradition alliance to working together in groups:

> Mexican sorcerers believe, to advance spiritually, people should work together in groups of nine or eleven. Indian scriptures indicate that some gurus of ancient times had eleven disciples. And then there was Jesus!
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.15, p.172

Pooling energy resources multiplies spiritual strength and makes seva more efficient. Gurudev's disciples who collaborated achieved more than any working alone.

### Role-Play

The guru's multi-role nature is more demanding than the disciple's. A disciple must play roles within family and social life; a guru must switch roles *within the spiritual relationship itself* — from guru to father to mother to friend — sometimes within a fraction of a second.

> Gurudev was a master of role-play with the ability to switch roles within a fraction of a second! We knew when he was playing father, we could have a sense of entitlement and make requests. Casualness was acceptable. But when playing guru, he was a formidable person to be in the company of, and you certainly needed to mind your Ps and Qs.
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.15, pp.514–517

The disciple's own role-play had to match the guru's chosen mode — which was itself a test and training. See [[concepts/role-play]].

## Succession by Merit

> Golden Principle: "Hum ansh se chalte hain, vansh se nahin."
> (Succession should be by merit and not birth.)
> — Gurudev, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.15, p.583

Spiritualism is a merit-based institution, not a family-run business. A guru's successor must have the qualities of a guru and be spiritually equipped to take the institution higher. Hingori notes three historical illustrations:

- **Guru Nanak Dev** did not pass his spiritual legacy to any of his sons but to Bhai Lehna, who became Guru Angad Dev.
- **Guru Charan Singh Ji** of the Radha Soami sect was succeeded by his nephew Gurinder Singh — a corporate executive at the time — not his son Rana, who was polished and educated but not interested.
- **Ashwatthama**, son of Guru Dronacharya, played a villain's role despite being a great archer and the son of a master — illustrating that the guru's child does not automatically inherit spiritual qualification.

> Often, people look for successors from within their own family. This is a view spiritualists do not subscribe to, since spiritualism is a merit-based institution, not a family-run business.
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.15, p.535

After Gurudev's passing, his wife — who had been in a supportive role for two decades — took on running the institution until her demise in 2014. Malhotraji was treated as the most senior, but not as guru. Gurudev had predicted his disciples would eventually operate independently, yet in collaboration.

## Shifting Gears: Disciple to Guru

The transition from disciple to guru does not happen by announcement. It happens organically through accumulated responsibility:

1. Senior disciples mentor the juniors — answering questions, counselling on infirmities, helping solve spiritual mysteries.
2. Those who do this effectively, inspiring their juniors, are given more responsibilities.
3. Such disciples slowly shift gears from being disciples to being **gurus-on-trial** (undesignated).
4. The intern who learns becomes the intern who teaches — a **guru-in-practice**, though not yet a guru-nominate.

> I would prefer a stumbling guru than a sturdy disciple (in a manner of speaking).
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.15, p.623

What Hingori finds missing in some sevadaars is "their intense desire to share and help others grow." This aspiration to teach and propagate is the essential distinguishing quality — without it, a disciple can reach the maximum of being a stellar disciple, but no further.

## Counterbalances

A guru ascending toward guruhood must be watchful of the *power of counterbalance* — forces that can destroy a person's ascent. These include:

- Weakness for the opposite sex
- Greed for wealth
- Hunger for self-projection
- Nepotism
- Inability to gracefully accept power (influence over others)

> So, a guru-in-making must be watchful of the counterbalances and tread the path with caution.
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.15, p.635

## Nuances

### The Mahurat of Disciples

A guru must sense the inner being of disciples and ignore some of their rough exterior qualities — because those rough qualities exist as a consequence of the mahurat of birth. Disciples could not always choose a birth timing that matched both their inner qualities and their guru's timeline.

> Golden Principle: If you are dissatisfied with your physical disposition or personality, do remember, what you received in exchange may be a greater gift of greater relevance.
> — Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.15, p.89

The disciple chose to synchronise with the guru's lifetime and accepted whatever planetary conditions came with the available birth window. The guru's task is to look past the mahurat-driven exterior and recognise the inner being. See [[concepts/mahurat]].

### The Double Confession

This chapter contains a rare double admission. First, Hingori confesses that in his early years as a guru he selected disciples based on spiritual obsession rather than thorough assessment of qualities and gunas. Second, he admits he was harsher toward disciples making the same mistakes he once made than he was toward himself at their stage. The maturity of a guru includes owning these early failures rather than rationalising them.

### Saint vs. Guru

A saint's evolution concerns only himself; a guru manages the evolution of many people across generations. This distinction is what *requires* the guru to be shrewd, tactful, and collaborating — qualities a saint may never need.

## In Practice

- When a disciple errs, the guru's response is calibrated to the stage of the disciple's journey, not the guru's current standard.
- A guru watches his own progress: forgiving more readily at the conscious level, while observing at the subconscious level what the energy is doing to those who misuse it.
- Role-matching: the disciple signals which role they need; the guru reads it and responds accordingly.
- Merit-based succession requires the guru to resist family and social pressure to anoint successors by birth or seniority.
