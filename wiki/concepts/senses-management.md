# Senses Management

## Definition

Senses management is the practice of governing the five senses — sight, hearing, touch, taste, smell — not by suppression but by *observation*, so that the practitioner becomes an observer of the sensory play rather than the actor who indulges in it. This detachment allows the senses to be used as tools rather than rulers.

*Witnessing Greatness* Ch.7 frames the starting point plainly: human sensory perception is fundamentally flawed. We detect only about 0.00000000000035% of the electromagnetic spectrum, cannot hear dog-whistle frequencies, perceive solid objects that are mostly empty space at the atomic level, and miss scent molecules that animals detect with ease. Despite this, these senses form the foundation of our perceptions, which mould our beliefs, thoughts, and actions — and our limited perceptions make us mistake our narrow slice of experience for the whole of reality. This is what Eastern philosophy calls [[concepts/maya|Maya]]. The senses, then, are not neutral instruments; they are built-in filters that guarantee a personalised, contracted view of reality. Senses management is the deliberate work of loosening that contraction.

> Gurudev learned to manage his senses not by suppressing them but by observing his reactions as they played out. In this manner, he became an observer of the sensory play rather than the actor who indulged in them. By being an observer, the mahaguru immediately created a template for controlling and manipulating his senses as per his will.
> — *The Guru of Gurus*, p.172

This is distinct from asceticism or mortification. It is a shift in *position* — from actor to observer — that allows full engagement with the world while remaining unbound by it. See [[concepts/role-play]] for how this same posture shapes Gurudev's emotional functioning.

## Why the Senses Bind

The brain has no direct interface with the outside world. It interprets electrochemical signals from the sensory organs. As a result, *what we see is an internal model of our personal world, not what is out there.* Our perceived reality is a more personalised view of reality and not the cosmic reality. To align with the cosmic perspective, you must overcome the limitations of a personalised worldview — which begins with managing the senses.

> Multiple senses working together give rise to a series of emotions leading to a range of actions.
> — *The Guru of Gurus*, p.172

## Gurudev's Methods, Sense by Sense

### Sight

The mahaguru's formula for dealing with the opposite gender: imagine every attractive woman simultaneously in her *gudiya* (child) and *budiya* (aged) forms to accurately evaluate attraction. Hingori went further, visualising every woman he met as a skeleton — insurance against untoward desire that also revealed *"beauty was literally in the mind of the beholder."*

*Witnessing Greatness* Ch.7 adds that Gurudev taught this technique to those who approached him grappling with challenges about their perceptions of the opposite gender. He cautioned that excessive focus on the opposite gender could adversely affect spiritual well-being by draining spiritual energy and weakening the aura — the eyes, he said, are not merely windows to the soul but can attract and emit energy. Virender ji summarises the result of Gurudev's own achievement in this domain: *"In Gurudev, I saw the finest self-control. He wasn't attracted by money or women or praise. Nothing affected him. I never saw any lust in him. That particular trait was alien to him."* (*Witnessing Greatness*, Ch.7, p.94)

Gurudev believed one should not be overly drawn to beauty or repulsed by a lack of it — the [[concepts/vasna|vaasnas]] (engrams in the causal body) that surface must be transcended, not fed.

Gurudev stated that when evolved spiritualists die, their spirits arrive at a crossway — one path leads to lovely-looking lower lokas, the other to plainer-looking higher lokas. The pursuit of beauty can land a spirit in a lower realm. Hingori's own journeys confirmed this: *"The higher realms were bright yet sparsely inhabited, a stark contrast to the lower realm, which felt like a dark, seedy nightclub."* (*Witnessing Greatness*, Ch.7, p.95) See [[concepts/loks]].

### Smell

Taking a leaf from Aghor vidya: *develop an indifference to stench if you want to cultivate an indifference to incense.* Hingori practised tolerance and nonchalance to unpleasant smells; over time he also gave up colognes and perfumes. The sense of smell as a teacher in both directions.

*Witnessing Greatness* Ch.7 confirms: Gurudev viewed sensory experiences differently than most. He never wore cologne — he felt true spiritual growth meant not being swayed by life's dualities, including what we find pleasant or unpleasant.

### Taste

Gurudev's own food journey: foodie in youth → sattvic by the time of his ministry. Lassi (buttermilk) for lunch on most days; nimbu pani (lemonade) for breakfast. On Mahashivratri, he did not eat until the last person in the queue had been met — which could take two days.

> "He who learns to feed others before feeding himself transcends the desire for food."
> — Gurudev to his daughter, *The Guru of Gurus*, p.171

He also recommended fasting once a week as a detox that reduces bodily secretions and frees the mind from activity and stress.

An anecdote illustrates sensory management through taste: Gurudev once visited an associate's home with disciples. The associate and wife were absent; their eight-year-old daughter offered to make tea and brewed it on a kerosene stove. Upon leaving, the mahaguru gave his disciples a sharp mouthful for barely sipping the kerosene-smelling tea: *"Is it possible they lost a lot because they could not bear a little?"*

**Multiple witnesses on Gurudev's food mastery (*Witnessing Greatness* Ch.7)**

*Witnessing Greatness* Ch.7 adds a chorus of disciple testimonies on the same theme. Bittu ji: *"To him, the type of dish or its freshness was not a priority. He often picked food from the previous day, even with fresh options."* Puran ji, who spent many years in Gurudev's company, observed: *"Gurudev readily accepted any vegetarian food cooked for him, no matter how simple. He never demanded particular dishes. His spiritual path had overshadowed his physical needs."* Alka ji articulates the principle: *"For Daddy, the significance of food lay not in its taste or flavour, but in the intention and thoughtfulness behind its preparation."* Raji Sharma ji adds texture: *"Gurudev was easy-going about food. Whether it was parathas, a slice of bread, or even mathi with tea, he gracefully accepted everything. While he had a soft spot for certain snacks such as gol-gappas, he was not driven by cravings."*

Hingori notes that early tales from Gaggu ji show young Gurudev enjoying flavourful Indian meals — chole bhature, pinnis. Between the 1970s and 1980s a clear shift occurred: not from pleasure to aversion, but to achieve mastery over the sense of taste.

Gurudev also often went days with just tea and no food. He would bring food to his mouth, then decide against eating. When Bittu ji challenged him on this:

> Bittu ji: "You have always emphasised the importance of respecting food. Today, you are not adhering to that principle."
>
> Gurudev: "When I am on the verge of eating, are my eyes shut or open?"
>
> Bittu ji: "They are shut."
>
> Gurudev: "Before I take a bite, I shut my eyes and scan to ensure that all my spiritual children have been fed. If even one remains hungry, I find it hard to eat. Their hunger weighs on me."
> — *Witnessing Greatness*, Ch.7, p.92

See also the GoG version of this teaching in [[stories/gurudev-not-eating-hungry-disciples]]. Renu ji preserves the crystallising phrase: *"Jennu khellaan che mazaa aa gaya na, khaane da mazaa nahin rehnda"* — when one experiences the joy of feeding others, the pleasure of feeding oneself pales in comparison. The occasion was Indu Sharma ji, a central figure in the Gurgaon kitchen, noticing that Daddy had not eaten dinner the previous night.

**Food as a spiritual carrier: cook's emotion and intention**

Indian philosophy holds that a dish prepared by two different individuals carries distinct energies affecting those who consume it. A cook's emotions and intentions are transferred into their cooking — which is why temple cooks are meticulously selected. Hingori noticed this in both directions: meals prepared by Mataji at the Gurgaon sthan carried *"a distinctive essence — a difference those attuned to sensing such energies felt."* Conversely, Sitaram, a domestic helper at his parents' house who frequently used colourful language, inadvertently transferred that quality to those who ate food he prepared; Hingori found his own speech beginning to reflect Sitaram's style.

**Food, karmic debt, and black magic**

Gurudev taught that one should not feel compelled to consume food financed by others to avoid incurring a [[concepts/karmic-debt|karmic debt]], and not to select food solely based on its flavour. Light-coloured foods (white dishes) are particularly receptive to subtle environmental energies — which makes them potential vehicles for negative practices. Hingori recounts: before his encounter with Gurudev, he fell victim to such an attack. Someone channelled harmful energy towards him via white pedas charged through specific rituals and reverse mantras, contributing to his severe arthritis. Protective measure: add spices such as turmeric or black pepper to white or light-coloured foods to change their colour and pre-empt negative energies. See [[concepts/spiritual-attacks]].

### Hearing

The sense of hearing connects us to others in ways no other sense can. Gurudev discouraged idle talk and gossip. His discipline: develop discernment — intuitively hear the unsaid, and be selective in responding to what is heard. *"Do not believe anything we hadn't seen or heard for ourselves."*

A celebrated anecdote on discernment: when a disciple denied a mistake in front of Gurudev, the mahaguru had Bittu ji fetch a sealed set of blank cassettes from a bedside cabinet and play the third one. Those seated heard the verbatim first-person conversation of the disciple bragging about the same 'mistake' he had vehemently denied. See [[stories/blank-cassette-disciple]].

Gurudev liked Mohammed Rafi and radio in the car; over time his interest in music waned to the point where he could appreciate it without being drawn into it or drowning in its melody.

*Witnessing Greatness* Ch.7 gives more detail on this progression. When young, Gurudev enjoyed music like many — whistling popular tunes or playing them on a mouth organ, and especially loving the classic songs from the black-and-white era featured on the radio show *Binaca Geet Mala*. As he progressed spiritually, his relationship with auditory stimuli changed: from simply enjoying sound, he used his sense of hearing as a spiritual tool. He could hear thoughts and perceive spoken words from great distances. A road trip Hingori took with his father from Chandigarh to Gurgaon illustrates this: the journey was fraught with tension, with father and son arguing most of the way. Upon arriving, Gurudev gently but promptly admonished Hingori for his behaviour, fully aware of the entire exchange. *"His vigilance was such that nothing went unnoticed."* (*Witnessing Greatness*, Ch.7, p.95)

### Touch / Anger / Temperature

High temper in powerful spiritualists is attributable to the uprisen fire element owing to intense tapasya. Gurudev's method in early years at IARI: he sat in the corridor outside his office abusing passersby. When they retaliated, he smiled back — deliberately inciting abuse to test his resilience. He erased a strong emotion by inducing it until it lost its grip. *Lesser the emotion, more the stability of the mind.*

*Witnessing Greatness* Ch.7 adds the temperature dimension. While disciples layered themselves to stay warm during the cold winters in the hills, Gurudev's comfort in merely a sweater illustrated his mastery. His control extended beyond his own body to the physical sensations of those around him: at a camp at Mungaoli, Madhya Pradesh, Hingori wore only a T-shirt in the cold. Apprehensive of Gurudev's reaction upon his return, he quickly put on a sweater — only to find himself shivering uncontrollably. Gurudev's simple question, *"Why did you do that?"* miraculously stopped the shivering, *"showcasing not just his control over his own physical sensations but also his influence on the physical sensations of those around him."* (*Witnessing Greatness*, Ch.7, p.96) The episode also taught that the senses, though strong, can be influenced in ways we might not realise; and that pretence has immediate physical costs. See [[stories/mungaoli-sweater-incident]].

### Water as Sensory-Spiritual Medium

*Witnessing Greatness* Ch.7 gives a dedicated treatment of water that extends the senses-management framework. Water reflects the emotions and intentions of those interacting with it: despite retaining its chemical composition, its molecular structure can change, manifesting a dual potential to heal and harm. This makes it both a powerful healing tool in spiritual practice and a potential vehicle for black magic (such as the case of Kamlesh, a young girl from Gurgaon who unknowingly drank sherbet mixed with a harmful taweez; Gurudev intervened in time — see [[stories/kamlesh-black-magic-cure]]).

At Gurudev's sthans, jal (water) is used for healing. It is infused with powerful mantras, making it a medium of the mahaguru's spiritual energy. Gurudev also used water as a vehicle for direct mantra transfer: he would sometimes sip from a glass of water and then pass the rest to a disciple as a way of sharing his spiritual energy and qualities with that person — sometimes to cure, sometimes to advance their spiritual journey. He would say *"I've transferred 10,000 recitations of this mantra to you"* while sharing his sipped water, indicating he was imparting the accumulated benefit of his own mantra practice. On special days such as Guru Purnima, disciples would wash Gurudev's feet with water and then drink that water infused with his spiritual energy — a way to receive the mahaguru's blessings and deepen the bond. See [[concepts/mantra-transfer]].

### Managing Sight — The Young Devotee's Tea

Gurudev once blessed the tea offered by an eight-year-old hostess and handed the cups to his disciples. Some barely sipped and left the rest because it smelled of kerosene. The mahaguru's rebuke was direct: could they not endure a small discomfort for the sake of what had been offered and blessed?

## Neuroplasticity

When the brain is denied input in one sensory modality, it can rewire itself to support and augment other senses. The areas of the brain dedicated to the lost sense are remapped to process other senses, sometimes including the birth of new neurons.

Gurudev instructed Hingori to open a school for the deaf in Dehradun, which Hingori eventually did. Working with the deaf revealed their exceptional manual dexterity and high concentration — seldom distracted by sound, their sense of touch is dramatically enhanced.

> The mahaguru suggested we let one vice remain till we manage to control or offset the others. Ultimately, we must give up even the remaining vice.
> — *The Guru of Gurus*, p.173

Hidden in this simple suggestion were *the secrets of neuroplasticity* — progressive sensory control, with the last vice serving as an anchor while the others are progressively relinquished. Hingori identifies his remaining challenge as the sense of taste.

## The Mind

The brain is a multisensory organ blending information from various senses. The mind — distinct from the brain — is the spirit and causal-body counterpart. It provides extrasensory information to the brain, granting access to psychic abilities: intuition, telepathy, remote sensing, clairvoyance, clairaudience, psychokinesis.

The physical body is not solid matter but vibrating strings of energy — seven octillion atoms made of energy vortices, each with a distinct energy impression. As a result, the universe is nothing more than vibrating strings of energy, consistent with quantum physics' findings.

To change the state of your consciousness, you must change the level of your mind. The higher the level of the mind, the lesser its movement and the greater your awareness. When the mind is stiller, it reflects the param-aatma to a greater degree.

## In a Nutshell

> The form exists in the seeing. The sound exists in the hearing. The feel exists in the touching. The taste exists in the tasting. The smell exists in the smelling. If a person can diminish the experience of seeing, hearing, touching, tasting, and smelling, then there isn't much to experience. At this stage, the experiencer begins to experience himself. In that experience of himself, he grasps his limitlessness and realises, as is he, so is the experience.
> — *The Guru of Gurus*, p.175

Sensory management is ultimately the method by which the jivaatma strips away the personalised worldview and begins to perceive its own nature.
