# Hingori at Badrinath — "Hello Brother!"

**Source:** *Aatma Sutra*, Ch.XIII, pp.159–160

## Full Narrative

> Thank god, Gurudev was not a great cricketer and especially not a brilliant bowler! Else, he would never have bowled a straight ball, only googlies. He asked me to spend a few days with him at his camp in Srinagar, Uttarakhand. There were other disciples in the camp, and he decided to take us to the famous Badrinath Temple.
>
> On reaching the temple, Gurudev waited outside while he asked me to go inside, hug the deity and say, "*My dear elder brother, I have come to give thee a hug.*" In Punjabi it sounds more emphatic though slightly comical, "*Bade Bhaiya main twanu jhappi paan ayaan*." I went inside not knowing whom to hug but was certain he did not want me to hug the temple deity. I chanced upon a statue of Adi Shankaracharya, the founder of the temple. I found him easy to hug. He was tangible, measurable, and huggable. I quite enjoyed hugging his marble statue. Call it a spiritual version of a chilled-out experience.
>
> When I came out and gave Gurudev an account of what I had done, he sent me back in and asked me to mentally hug Lord Badrinath. Whew! Was I to go and hug the deity of one of the most powerful temples in the country? It did not make sense to me at all, but Gurudev was dead serious. It took me years to figure it out. It was one of the reasons that could have led to this book.
>
> What Gurudev told me, without voicing it, was that I should consider myself a younger brother to that very powerful force. He expected me to believe in the equality of one *aatma* with another. He knew this was going to be a huge concept for me to fathom, and yet he wanted me to do so. I certainly felt elevated that he considered me a brother to such an icon.
>
> I also found it justifiable to accept the concept of self-worship. My inferiority complexes were shaken and shattered!

## *Witnessing Greatness* Version (Ch.8, p.108)

> Once, Gurudev invited me to join him at his camp in Srinagar, Uttarakhand. From there, we embarked on a journey to the famous Badrinath temple. Upon our arrival, he gave me an unexpected instruction: "Go inside, embrace the deity, and say, 'Dear brother, I am here to hug you.'"
>
> I was perplexed. How could I, a mere mortal, address such a revered deity as my brother? Filled with self-doubt yet trusting Gurudev's guidance, I entered the temple and softly uttered those words. It was the moment the mahaguru gently ushered me into the concept of aatmic equality. It did not lead to an immediate transformation; more than a decade passed before clarity replaced my confusion. I am now convinced that, at our very core, none of us is inherently superior or inferior to another. This insight has led me to consider myself a spiritual communist, perhaps the first of my kind!
> — Hingori, *Witnessing Greatness*, Ch.8, p.108

Ch.8 frames the episode within Gurudev's practice of sending disciples to temples "for spiritual outreach and alliance-building" (see [[concepts/spiritual-alliances]]). It names the insight **aatmic equality**, gives "more than a decade" for the clarity to come, and coins "spiritual communist." The *Aatma Sutra* detour to the Adi Shankaracharya statue is not mentioned.

## Related Concepts

The story is the biographical seed of the entire concept of self-worship in *Aatma Sutra*. Hingori acknowledges: "It took me years to figure it out. It was one of the reasons that could have led to this book." The Badrinath instruction — equality of one *aatma* with another — is the lived experience behind the philosophical argument.

Hingori's instinct to hug the Shankaracharya statue (tangible, measurable, huggable) rather than the abstract deity is also characteristic: he works from the concrete and visible toward the invisible.
