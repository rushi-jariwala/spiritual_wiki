# Hingori Controls Rain — and Punctures Tyres

**Source:** Hingori, *Guru Sutra*, Ch.13, pp.143–145

## Rain Control

> Years ago, an incident happened when the car I was driving suddenly came to a halt. It was raining heavily and I was engrossed in reciting the Mahagayatri Mantra. I wished for the rain to stop and it instantly did. I was unsure if it was my imagination or just an unusual coincidence. Anyhow, I felt guilty and so willed the rain to start again, and surprisingly, it did. On second thought, I felt if it did not stop raining, water would get into the carburetor making it difficult for me to restart the car. And so, yet again, I willed the rain to stop, and stop it did. By then, I was sure this was the manifestation of the power of the mantra. I promised myself to never abuse the power again.

## Mazhar Khan's Tyre

> Later that day, while driving back from the city to the suburbs, I noticed an acquaintance, the late Mazhar Khan, driving just ahead of me. I wished for him to stop. Instantly, his car tyre punctured, and the car came to an abrupt halt. I got the opportunity to meet him after several years and we chatted by the kerbside.

## Own Tyre — The Foolish Experiment

> Gurudev had asked me to drive his office supervisor to Dehradun. I did as asked and recited the Mahagayatri Mantra while driving. I wondered if the mantra could puncture my car's tyre and so I willed it to. A few seconds later, I had to bring my car to a screeching halt. I felt foolish because I had to change the tyre myself. I recognise that I had used the power Gurudev had vested in me in a counter productive way.

**Context:** Three consecutive demonstrations of the same power: (1) elemental command over rain — starting and stopping it at will; (2) directional will causing a tyre puncture in another's vehicle to create a reunion; (3) willing his own tyre to puncture as an experiment, resulting in roadside inconvenience. Together they form Hingori's candid self-portrait of early intoxication with acquired power.

The guilt about stopping rain is immediate and self-correcting. The tyre puncture of Mazhar Khan's car, though benign in effect (a pleasant reunion), was still wilful use of the mantra for a personal preference. The self-inflicted puncture is the most honest: testing the mantra's limits at personal cost, then recognising the foolishness of it.

These accounts exist not as boasts but as instructional examples — Hingori explicitly says he did not allow them to add to his guilt quotient but uses them to teach the necessity of checks and balances.
