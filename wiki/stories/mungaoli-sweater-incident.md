# The Mungaoli Sweater

## Full Narrative

> He made me chatter and shiver with chills down my spine because I wore a sweater upon his arrival. I didn't want him to think I was bragging about not being cold when almost everyone else was snug in their cardigans. By turning my pretence into reality, he demonstrated his control not only over my mind and body but also over nature. I soon realised that surrender to the mahaguru included the sacrifice of false ego, social graces, and make-believe.
>
> Life in these camps was no walk in the park. When Gurudev's team couldn't find a suitable location, they often pitched tents on open grounds. Whether it was his bachelorhood room of 120-square-feet, the decrepit district guest houses, or the makeshift tents of his camp stays, the mahaguru was unconcerned about his living conditions.
>
> I eventually understood that mundane aspects of life neither perturbed nor distracted him. Furthermore, by being away from his family for extended periods, the mahaguru lived the life of a renunciate despite being a grihasth. His words, "Grihasth mein bhi vairagya hai", qualified this sentiment.
> — Hingori, *The Guru of Gurus*, pp.129–130

## Source

*The Guru of Gurus*, Entrepreneur Extraordinaire — Offsites, pp.129–130

## Context

This episode occurred at one of Gurudev's biannual offsite seva camps. Hingori wore a sweater partly out of social vanity — not wanting to appear as if he was showing off by going without. Gurudev read the pretence and made the cold real, stripping away the affectation and leaving Hingori shivering in his sweater. The teaching landed immediately: surrender includes surrendering the small social performances that prop up the ego. The episode closes with Gurudev's ek vakya: *"Grihasth mein bhi vairagya hai"* — there is renunciation within the householder life too.

## Cross-Source Variant — *Witnessing Greatness* Ch.7

*Witnessing Greatness* Ch.7, p.95 retells the same event with complementary detail:

> Despite the cold, I initially wore only a T-shirt. However, upon Gurudev's return, apprehensive of his reaction, I quickly put on a sweater, only to find myself shivering uncontrollably. His simple question, "Why did you do that?" miraculously stopped my shivering, showcasing not just his control over his own physical sensations but also his influence on the physical sensations of those around him.
> — Hingori, *Witnessing Greatness*, Ch.7, p.95

Both accounts converge on the same teaching: social pretence — whether putting a sweater on to appear appropriately dressed or to avoid seeming to brag — attracted Gurudev's correction. The WG version adds a dimension absent from *The Guru of Gurus*: Gurudev's *question* was itself the instrument. He did not re-command Hingori's body by exertion — a single enquiry was sufficient. This reveals not only control over his own physical sensations but active influence over the physical sensations of those around him.

> [!tip] A single spoken question stopping uncontrollable shivering implies the reverse is also true: his presence could induce or amplify physical sensation just as easily. The camp's cold became real because Hingori wore the sweater; the shivering stopped because Gurudev's question called Hingori back to honesty.
